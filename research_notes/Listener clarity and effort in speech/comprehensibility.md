# Being understood vs. how hard it is to understand: intelligibility, comprehensibility and listener effort

Scope note: builds on `reports/Proven ways to speak articulately.md`, which covered pause location, speech rate, fillers, hedges, task repetition and feedback design. Nearly all comprehensibility research is on second-language (L2) speakers rated by native listeners. Tags: **Strong** = meta-analysis; **Moderate** = several agreeing studies; **Single** = one study; **L2** = L2-speaker evidence only. Some sources were read only as abstracts or search snippets; this is flagged where it applies.

## 1. The Derwing & Munro framework: definitions, measurement, independence

### Takeaway
Three separate listener measures. **Intelligibility** is whether the words got through (scored from what the listener writes down). **Comprehensibility** is how easy the listener *felt* it was (a rating scale). **Accentedness** is how different it sounds. They are related but partly independent (**Strong, L2**). The owner's insight lines up exactly with comprehensibility vs. intelligibility. Across 49 reports, they correlate only r = .57, so speech can be fully understood and still be hard work.

### Cited Findings
- Munro & Derwing (1995, *Language Learning*) introduced the three dimensions and found them "related but partially independent." Heavily accented speech can be highly intelligible. — [Munro & Derwing "redux" chapter](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/bct.121.02mun); [FORRT replication atlas entry for the 1995 paper](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1995.tb00963.x/)
- **Intelligibility** is actual comprehension, usually measured by having listeners transcribe an utterance and scoring the share of words they got right. Comprehension questions are also used. **Comprehensibility** is a scalar judgement of how easy the utterance was perceived to be. Listeners can transcribe accurately while rating the speech hard to follow. — [Munro & Derwing redux](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/bct.121.02mun); [JSLP 2020 retrospective](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/jslp.20038.mun)
- Kennedy & Trofimovich (2008) define comprehensibility as "listeners' perceptions of how easily they understand an utterance." Native-speaker sentences were always rated more comprehensible than L2 sentences. — [Kennedy & Trofimovich 2008 PDF](https://www.paveltrofimovich.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Kennedy-Trofimovich-2008.pdf)
- **Meta-analysis, Chau & Huensch (2025, *SSLA* 47(1), 282–307):** 49 reports (1995–2023), 141 effect sizes, three-level models. Weighted mean correlations:
  - fluency–comprehensibility **.82**
  - comprehensibility–accentedness **.75**
  - fluency–accentedness **.62**
  - intelligibility–comprehensibility **.57**
  - intelligibility–accentedness **.32**

  Moderators: controlled tasks (e.g. reading aloud) produced *higher* correlations than spontaneous ones. When the same listeners rated several dimensions, the fluency–comprehensibility and comprehensibility–accentedness links were weaker. — [Chau & Huensch 2025 PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BF27ACC694B94D3B99D8BC6D05BFCA54/S0272263125000014a.pdf/the-relationships-among-l2-fluency-intelligibility-comprehensibility-and-accentedness-a-meta-analysis.pdf); [UCSB news summary](https://ems.ucsb.edu/news/tuc-chau-publishes-studies-second-language-acquisition) (numbers taken from the abstract, via search; the full PDF did not render)
- An L2 Spanish conceptual replication found partial independence again, but proficiency may mediate how error rates map onto the dimensions. — [search summary of KPFU archive record](https://dspace.kpfu.ru/xmlui/handle/net/180739?show=full) (snippet only)
- Levis, Derwing & Munro (eds., 2022), *The Evolution of Pronunciation Teaching and Research: 25 Years of Intelligibility, Comprehensibility, and Accentedness*. — [catalogue record](https://vufind.lib.tsu.ru/Record/libtsu.1013743/Description)

### Inferences
- For the app: "Was it understood?" maps to intelligibility, which a fluent native speaker almost always passes. "How much work was it?" maps to comprehensibility, the construct to target. The r = .57 overlap means the two are not interchangeable even in L2 speech, where intelligibility actually varies. For fluent speakers, intelligibility is near ceiling, so comprehensibility (effort) is the only one left that varies.
- Fluency–comprehensibility at r = .82 is the strongest link in the field. It is partly a halo effect, because the link weakens when the same rater scores both. The app should not treat its fluency numbers and a comprehensibility score as independent evidence.
- Controlled tasks inflate correlations. Evidence from spontaneous tasks, which is what the app records, is the more relevant subset.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect sizes split by task type (spontaneous vs. read) were not extractable. The PDF would not parse.
- No equivalent framework or meta-analysis exists for native/fluent speakers.

## 2. Linguistic correlates of comprehensibility beyond pronunciation, including discourse and organization

### Takeaway
Comprehensibility depends on many things: fluency, vocabulary, grammar and discourse. Accentedness depends mostly on pronunciation (**Strong, L2**). Discourse-level features matter most for *more proficient* speakers and in *complex* tasks (**Moderate, L2**). The best recent evidence is Tsunemoto & Trofimovich (2024): rated coherence correlates r = .70 with comprehensibility. Among the measured features, **more additive "and" connectives predicted lower comprehensibility**, a direct signature of rambling. This is the most relevant finding for the owner's case.

### Cited Findings
- **Isaacs & Trofimovich (2012, *SSLA* 34(3), 475–505), "Deconstructing comprehensibility":** 40 French learners of English, 19 speech measures (segmental, suprasegmental, fluency, lexical, grammatical, *discourse*), rated by 60 native English listeners. A sister paper on the same data (*Bilingualism: Language and Cognition* 15(4)) found accent tied mainly to phonology (rhythm, segment and syllable accuracy) and comprehensibility tied mainly to grammatical accuracy and lexical richness. — [SSLA article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/deconstructing-comprehensibility/6652F801054175C4C96F38DB2D40078C); [Disentangling accent from comprehensibility](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/disentangling-accent-from-comprehensibility/) (abstracts only)
- In the same line of work, lexical richness and fluency separated low-level learners, while **grammatical and discourse-level measures separated high-level learners**. — [Applied Psycholinguistics, learners at different ability levels](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/second-language-speech-production-investigating-linguistic-correlates-of-comprehensibility-and-accentedness-for-learners-at-different-ability-levels/9944A1B88B900F5C65EEE9040DC2D7CD) (abstract via search summary)
- **Crowther, Trofimovich, Saito & Isaacs (2018, *SSLA*):** 10 listeners, 60 speakers, 3 tasks. The linguistic dimensions behind comprehensibility vary by task. In the cognitively complex TOEFL-style task, the split between accentedness and comprehensibility shrank, and overlapping phonology, lexis and grammar variables contributed. — [SSLA abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/linguistic-dimensions-of-l2-accentedness-and-comprehensibility-vary-across-speaking-tasks/77720DD72F6673584405559D26AF7539); [UCL record](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1566736)
- **Saito, Trofimovich & Isaacs (2017, *Applied Linguistics*):** a validation and generalisation study of linguistic influences on comprehensibility in extemporaneous L2 speech. — [UCL record](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1525578) (abstract only; details not extracted)
- **Saito (2021, *TESOL Quarterly* 55(3), 866–900), meta-analysis:** 37 listener studies and 17 training studies. Comprehensibility drew on a wider range of features (segmental, prosodic, temporal); accentedness leaned on consonant and vowel accuracy. Instruction improved comprehensibility more than nativelikeness. — [UCL record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10125305/) (abstract only; pooled r values not extracted, PDF returned 403)
- **Suzuki & Kormos (2020, *SSLA* 42), argumentative L2 speech:** comprehensibility was predicted by articulation rate, mid-clause pause duration and ratio, morphological error rate and syllable-structure error rate. — [corrigendum with regression table](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/linguistic-dimensions-of-comprehensibility-and-perceived-fluency-an-investigation-of-complexity-accuracy-and-fluency-in-second-language-argumentative-speech-corrigendum/0E1DA47C1D2F30E35A56AF0FB8BD04B6)
- **Tsunemoto & Trofimovich (2024, *SSLA* 46(3), 795–817), "Coherence and comprehensibility":**
  - Design: 60 one-minute TOEFL-style integrated responses rated by 12 EAP instructors on unnumbered 100-point sliders. ICC = .87 (coherence), .88 (comprehensibility), .89 (accentedness).
  - Coherence correlated with comprehensibility at **r = .70** [.66, .74]. It stayed a significant predictor after controlling for accentedness, speaker L1 and rater accent familiarity. The model explained marginal R² = .72.
  - Comprehensibility was predicted by verb overlap (positive) and **additive connectives such as "and" (negative: about −2.7 points on a 100-point scale per extra connective per 100 words)**.
  - Coherence was predicted by structure and ordering of task-essential parts, verb overlap, and similarity to the source.
  - Signposting, causal and contrastive connectives, noun overlap and pronoun measures predicted neither. — [SSLA article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/coherence-and-comprehensibility-in-second-language-speakers-academic-speaking-performance/85221E116322A1A7ACD84242C454712A)
- Saito & Akiyama (L2 Japanese): comprehensibility tracked lexical appropriateness, pitch accent, speech rate and lexical variation; segmental and morphological errors mattered little. — [JSLP](https://benjamins.com/catalog/jslp.3.2.02sai) (search summary)
- A counterpoint: in Japanese EFL speech (Mikami 2024 dissertation), pronunciation accuracy and speech rate outweighed lexicogrammar. — [Nagoya thesis PDF](https://nagoya.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2010009/files/k14780_thesis.pdf) (search summary)

### Inferences
- For a fluent native speaker, pronunciation, grammar and lexical accuracy sit at ceiling. The literature's pattern ("higher proficiency → discourse and organization decide comprehensibility") suggests **organization and coherence are the levers left**. That fits the app's existing "clear point" judge.
- The "and"-connective finding gives a countable, plain-code proxy for rambling, in line with the CLAUDE.md rule "never use AI for anything that can be counted". The measure is additive connectives per 100 words. It comes from one L2 study, so treat it as a candidate metric to validate with evals, not a proven one.
- Signposting ("first", "second") did *not* predict either construct in that study. Teaching explicit signposts alone may not reduce effort. Ordering of the essential content did help coherence.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures coherence or organization's effect on comprehensibility in *native* spontaneous speech with the same methods.
- Effect sizes for Saito (2021) and Isaacs & Trofimovich (2012) were not extracted (paywalled or blocked PDFs).
- No study found of "task fulfilment" (answering the actual question) as a separate predictor of comprehensibility, apart from Tsunemoto & Trofimovich's "task-essential components".

## 3. Comprehensibility / listening effort with native or fluent speakers

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on native speakers is thin. Two findings carry over. First, pupillometry shows **extra listening effort even when recognition is 100% accurate** (**Single/Moderate**, accent-based). Second, native disfluencies like "um" can *help* the listener's prediction and attention even as they lower impressions of the speaker. Effort and impression are not the same thing.

### Cited Findings
- McLaughlin & Van Engen (2020, *JASA* 147(2), EL151–EL156): native English listeners showed larger pupil dilation for Mandarin-accented than for American-accented English, *even when they recognized the speech accurately*. In the earlier version, listeners also rated the accented talker more effortful. — [WashU profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/task-evoked-pupil-response-for-accurately-recognized-accented-spe/); [WashU news](https://linguistics.washu.edu/news/task-evoked-pupil-response-accurately-recognized-accented-speech) (abstract-level)
- Brown et al. (2020): only pupillometry, not other measures, revealed rapid adaptation that reduced listening effort for fully intelligible non-native speech. — [WashU profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/rapid-adaptation-to-fully-intelligible-nonnative-accented-speech-/)
- A clear speaking style reduced cognitive effort and improved comprehension for both native and non-native listeners. — [UCL thesis record](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10085853) (search summary)
- Native disfluencies ("uh"/"um") bias listeners to expect new or unfamiliar referents. They can aid recognition of low-frequency words and improve recall, which means a benefit to comprehension. — [Bosker dissertation](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/321897/Bosker_DISS2015.pdf?sequence=1); [MPI publication](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item_2055375) (search summary)
- Brennan & Williams (1995, *JML* 34(3)): filled pauses and rising prosody lowered how knowledgeable listeners judged the speaker to be. — [Springer/secondary citation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02175503); [arXiv: um, er](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.01207) (secondary descriptions)

### Inferences
- Effort, the impression of competence, and comprehension can come apart. A few "ums" may not raise effort and may even help, yet still cost credibility. Rambling structure, by contrast, plausibly raises effort directly. This supports weighting organization above filler count in any "listener effort" score. The earlier report reached a similar view on fillers.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures listening effort (pupillometry, dual-task, self-rated) for *well-organized vs. rambling* native spontaneous speech. This is the core gap for the app's premise. The closest evidence is L2 coherence ratings and accent pupillometry.

## 4. Listening effort measures and processing fluency: does harder-to-process speech lower judgements of the speaker even when understood?

### Takeaway
Yes, with caveats. Holding content identical, harder-to-process audio lowered ratings of the speaker's intelligence and likability, and of the research's importance (**Single, 2 experiments**). Accent-based credibility effects (Lev-Ari & Keysar 2010) are well known but **replicate inconsistently**. Listening effort measures (pupil, dual-task, self-report) often fail to correlate with each other (**Moderate**), so "effort" is not one number.

### Cited Findings
- FUEL framework (Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016, *Ear and Hearing*): effort is "the deliberate allocation of mental resources to overcome obstacles in goal pursuit," shaped by task demand, motivation and capacity. Measures fall into self-report, behavioural (e.g. dual-task) and physiological (e.g. pupillometry). — [FUEL overview PDF](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-my/wp-content/uploads/sites/2512/2019/11/04130529/Pichora-Fuller-et-al.-2016-FUEL-overview.pdf)
- Alhanbali et al. (2019): listening-effort measures "rarely correlate with each other and sometimes result in contradictory findings." Pupil size, reaction time, skin conductance, alpha power and self-report loaded on different factors. Behavioural and subjective indices often do not align. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01749/pdf); [ASHA JSLHR 2018](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2018_JSLHR-H-17-0473) (search summaries)
- Newman & Schwarz (2018, *Science Communication*): identical conference talks and NPR interviews played at low vs. high audio quality (n ≈ 97 and 99). With poor audio, listeners rated the researcher as less intelligent and less likable and the research as less important. — [ANU record](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/good-sound-good-research-how-audio-quality-influences-perceptions/); [USC News](https://today.usc.edu/why-we-believe-something-audio-sound-quality)
- Lev-Ari & Keysar (2010, *JESP*): trivia statements were believed less when spoken in a foreign accent, attributed to processing difficulty. The effect persisted when the speaker was only relaying a native speaker's statement. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/speakers-foreign-accent-are-perceived-less-credible). **Contradicted/mixed:** Stocker (2017) did not always replicate it (per [Grabka & Lev-Ari CUNY 2021 abstract](https://www.cuny2021.io/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/CUNY_2021_abstract_20.pdf)). Lorenzoni et al. (2024) found no accent difference in the illusory-truth effect, "contrary to the processing fluency hypothesis" ([Lorenzoni et al. 2024 PDF](https://research.unipd.it/retrieve/6f000abe-fb72-49c3-8497-078b16891347/Lorenzoni_FASIllusoryTruth_JoC_2024.pdf)).
- General processing-fluency theory: Alter & Oppenheimer (2009, *Personality and Social Psychology Review*), "Uniting the tribes of fluency" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868309341564). Oppenheimer (2006) found needlessly complex vocabulary lowered judged author intelligence in *written* text — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1178). Both cited from prior knowledge and not re-fetched this round.

### Inferences
- The owner's claim ("understood, but effortful, costs you") has reasonable indirect support. Processing difficulty does lower judgements of the speaker. The cleanest evidence comes from audio quality and written text, though, not from rambling *structure*. Extending it to structure is plausible but untested.
- Because effort measures disagree, the app should pick one operational definition. The field's practical default is a rated "ease of understanding" (comprehensibility) scale.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests whether *disorganized but intelligible native speech* lowers perceived competence or credibility, independently of fillers.

## 5. How comprehensibility is measured in practice; could 2–3 friends do it?

### Takeaway
The standard is a 9-point scale (1 = very easy, 9 = very hard to understand) or a 100-point slider, averaged over 10–60 listeners. Ten or more raters give high group reliability (α or ICC ≈ .85–.90+). Individual raters are noisy and use the middle of long scales inconsistently. With 2–3 friends, the averaged score would be too unreliable for small differences. It could work for **comparing two versions of the same answer side by side**, which is an untested inference.

### Cited Findings
- Isaacs & Thomson (2013, *Language Assessment Quarterly* 10, 135–159): 20 experienced and 20 novice raters used 5-point or 9-point scales for comprehensibility, accentedness and fluency (38 speakers). Cronbach's α was high, with no difference by scale length or experience. Rasch analysis showed raters struggled to tell scale steps apart, especially mid-scale, and more so on the 9-point scale. — [UCL PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1527145/1/Isaacs_rater_experience_rating_scale_length.pdf) (search summary)
- Tsunemoto & Trofimovich (2024): 12 trained raters on 100-point sliders reached ICC .87–.89. — [SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/coherence-and-comprehensibility-in-second-language-speakers-academic-speaking-performance/85221E116322A1A7ACD84242C454712A)
- A generalizability-theory study found comprehensibility ratings reached .90 reliability with about **15 trained raters or 50 untrained crowdsourced raters**. — [NSF PAR PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10531380) (search summary; author not confirmed)
- Nagle (2019, *JSLP*): 54 Mechanical Turk raters gave excellent reliability for comprehensibility and fluency, slightly lower for accentedness. — [Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/jslp.18016.nag)
- In the LALM benchmark below, *individual* human raters agreed with the rest of the group at r = .46–.73 (leave-one-out). That is roughly the ceiling for one rater's reliability. — [ACL Anthology, Issa & Ali](https://aclanthology.org/people/elsayed-issa/unverified/) (abstract via search)
- Comprehensibility ratings from interlocutors are being tested as predictors of their perceived communicative experience (Nagle et al., 2023, *Applied Psycholinguistics*). — [Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/EE25F60705E6D615A5AA03F7A3D65100/S0142716423000073a.pdf/framing-second-language-comprehensibility-do-interlocutors-ratings-predict-their-perceived-communicative-experience.pdf) (PDF did not parse; results not extracted)

### Inferences
- A friends version for the app's evals: 2–3 friends each rate "how easy was this to follow?" on a 9-point scale, plus one forced choice ("which of these two versions was easier to follow?"). Pairwise choices sidestep the mid-scale confusion Isaacs & Thomson found. This would be a sanity check, not a measurement. It could fit the existing `evals/` labels.
- With single-rater reliability of about .5–.7, three raters give roughly .75–.85 by the Spearman-Brown formula. That is usable for large differences between clips, not for tracking small daily changes. This is my calculation, not a published figure.

### Gaps
- No published study found that validates a 2–3-rater comprehensibility protocol, or a pairwise "which is easier" design, for native speech.

## 6. Can an LLM or automated system predict human comprehensibility ratings?

### Takeaway
Only partly. Feature-based models of *pronunciation* reach r ≈ .8 with human comprehensibility ratings on L2 or disordered speech. Zero-shot audio LLMs are weak: in a 2026 benchmark, the best reached r = .28 on comprehensibility, and none matched humans. I found no study that predicts the *discourse-driven* comprehensibility of native speech from transcripts with an LLM.

### Cited Findings
- Issa & Ali (BEA 2026): 90 L2 samples rated by 10 native raters and 5 large audio-language models. Only Gemini correlated significantly with humans on comprehensibility (**r = .28**); Qwen2-Audio reached r = .32 on intelligibility. No model reached human leave-one-out agreement (.46–.73). All showed systematic biases (−9.3 to +13.2 points). — [ACL Anthology author page](https://aclanthology.org/people/elsayed-issa/unverified/) (abstract via search)
- Dong et al. (Interspeech 2026), "Can Speech LLMs approximate human ratings of accentedness and comprehensibility?": reports moderate correlations with humans, more favorable than BEA. — [papers.cool listing](https://papers.cool/venue/dong26c@interspeech_2026@ISCA) (title/snippet only; numbers not verified)
- Parikh et al. (SLaTE 2025 / arXiv 2026): zero-shot Qwen2-Audio-7B on 5,000 Speechocean762 utterances (accuracy, fluency, prosody, completeness, not comprehensibility). It showed "strong agreement within ±2 tolerance" but overpredicted low-quality speech and was imprecise on errors. — [arXiv 2601.16230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16230)
- Classical pronunciation-score models: r = .823 machine–human on comprehensibility, comparable to naive listeners' inter-rater r = .760. Goodness-of-Pronunciation scores reached r ≈ .81 on disordered speech. — [HAL, disordered speech GOP](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01316846); [UCL record](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10146594) (search summaries)

### Inferences
- Automated comprehensibility so far is mostly *pronunciation* comprehensibility, which is irrelevant to a fluent native speaker. The app's angle is a text-based LLM judge on structure, plus counted features (connectives, pause location, speech rate). That is closer to the Tsunemoto & Trofimovich predictors, but its agreement with human effort ratings is untested. It would have to be checked the way `evals:judge` checks the clear-point marks: a few hand-rated "ease of following" labels per clip.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests an LLM reading *transcripts* to predict human comprehensibility or listening-effort ratings.
- Dong et al. (2026) effect sizes not retrieved.
