# Rubrics and raters: how experts, laypeople and machines judge whether speech is clear and articulate

Scope note: this builds on `reports/Proven ways to speak articulately.md` (coaching, fillers, pauses, feedback). That report already covers filler and pause perception in depth, so this file only touches it where it bears on rating. Many numbers below come from abstracts or secondary summaries because the primary papers were paywalled or their PDFs could not be parsed. Each such case is flagged. The 2026 arXiv items are unreviewed preprints.

## 1. Validated oral communication rubrics: dimensions, reliability, and what predicts overall ratings

### Takeaway
The validated public-speaking instruments (NCA Competent Speaker form, PSCR, AAC&U VALUE) all share the same core: a clear central message or thesis, organization (intro, body, conclusion, transitions), supporting material, language fit for the audience, and vocal and physical delivery. Inter-rater reliability is only moderate for content dimensions (persuasion and word choice are the worst), and better for low-inference, observable dimensions such as vocal expression. All were built for prepared classroom speeches, not 60-second impromptu answers.

### Cited Findings
**Validated vs popular.** Validated here means a published development and test study or a psychometric manual: NCA Competent Speaker form (manual with a reliability, validity and bias report), PSCR (development and test study in *Communication Education*), PSCR+ (interrater dataset). The AAC&U VALUE rubric was built by faculty consensus and is explicitly not a validated grading instrument. Coaching frameworks (PREP, What/So What/Now What, BLUF) are popular, untested structures (see the earlier report).

**NCA Competent Speaker Speech Evaluation Form (Morreale, Moore, Surges-Tatum & Webster, 2nd ed., NCA 2007)**
- Developed in 1990 by the NCA Committee for Assessment and Testing with representatives of 12 institutions. The 2nd edition keeps the original competencies and criteria and is described as a "standardized and tested instrument" for higher-education public speaking — [GMU copy of the form](https://4va.gmu.edu/wp-content/uploads/The-NCA-Competent-Speaker-Speech-Evaluation-Form.docx); [holistic version](https://4va.gmu.edu/wp-content/uploads/The-NCA-Competent-Speaker-Holistic-Speech-Evaluation-Form.docx)
- Eight competencies: four on speech preparation and four on delivery — [UNE public speaking PDF](https://une.edu/pdfs/public-speaking)
- The manual includes a "Reliability, Validity and Bias Testing Report" (manual pp. 26–27, cited in a 2023 MEXTESOL Journal appendix). The coefficients were not retrieved — [MEXTESOL Journal](https://mextesol.net/journal/public/files/98be3d560e7475bacb300ba7887170c9.pdf)
- An independent campus use (Marshall University, CMM 103) computed Cohen's kappa between raters, and all scores met its minimum threshold of .70 — [Marshall assessment report 2022–23](https://www.marshall.edu/assessment/files/2023/12/CMM-103-Assessment-2022-2023-Final61.pdf)
- A 1993 critique says the form struggles to tell competence levels apart, relies on the teacher's subjective view rather than the audience's, and describes competencies in a culturally narrow way — [Basic Communication Course Annual vol. 5](https://ecommons.udayton.edu/bcca/vol5/iss1/15)

**Public Speaking Competence Rubric, PSCR (Schreiber, Paul & Shibley 2012, *Communication Education* 61(3), 205–233)**
- 11 competencies, used in many open textbooks as the standard for evaluating presentations — [Lumen Learning, speaking competencies](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-fmcc-english1/chapter/speaking-competencies/)
- Only one item covers nonverbal behavior. The PSCR+ (Willoughby, Hughes, Sterman & Blevins) adds 10 nonverbal items scored 0 (deficient) to 4 (advanced). Four raters scored four recorded talks, with agreement checked by rwg(j), awg(j) and ADM(j) indices. The authors call it "valid and reliable" and say minimal training is needed, but the dataset page gives no coefficients — [Dryad PSCR+ dataset](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.6m905qg6r)

**AAC&U Oral Communication VALUE rubric**
- Five criteria on four levels: Organization, Language, Delivery, Supporting Material, Central Message. Built for "presentations of sufficient length such that a central message is conveyed", so not for one short answer — [UF copy of the rubric](https://www.assessment.aa.ufl.edu/media/assessmentaaufledu/value-rubrics/oral_communication-value_rubric.pdf); [UCSD copy](https://vcsa.ucsd.edu/_files/assessment-evaluation/resources-and-toolkit/additional%20resources/rubrics/rubricOralCommunication.pdf)
- Central message at the top level is "precisely stated, appropriately repeated, memorable, and strongly supported". At the bottom level it "can be deduced, but is not explicitly stated." Organization is "specific introduction and conclusion, sequenced material within the body, and transitions." Delivery covers posture, gestures, eye contact and voice, and at the top level the speaker "uses few vocal fillers." Language must be "appropriate to the topic and audience, grammatical, clear, and free from bias" — [UF copy](https://www.assessment.aa.ufl.edu/media/assessmentaaufledu/value-rubrics/oral_communication-value_rubric.pdf); [FIU copy](https://provost.fiu.edu/apa/assessment/resources/rubrics-and-curriculum-maps/_assets/rubrics/Oral%20Communication%20Rubric%20-%20VALUE.pdf)
- AAC&U says the VALUE rubrics were written by faculty teams from existing campus rubrics and are "intended for institutional-level use in evaluating and discussing student learning, not for grading." It reports no reliability data — [AAC&U VALUE page](https://www.aacu.org/initiatives/value-initiative/value-rubrics/value-rubrics-oral-communication)
- An Indiana University pilot of the oral rubric found faculty ratings slightly lower than independent raters'. It gave no coefficients — [IUPUI assessment minutes, Jan 2016](https://planning.indianapolis.iu.edu/assessment/prac-files/minutes/2015-16/jan16minutes.pdf)
- For other VALUE rubrics, UT Arlington reports inter-rater reliability of .50–.62 (Quantitative Literacy) and .64–.70 (Critical Thinking) — [UTA "On Solid Ground"](https://cdn.prod.web.uta.edu/-/media/project/website/administration/ier/documents/core-assessment/on-solid-ground1.pdf)
- Szafran (2017) documents a case where VALUE rubric reliability was misreported and, once corrected, judged inadequate. He warns against stopping rater training before raters show satisfactory reliability — [PARE, Szafran 2017](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/pare/article/id/1630)

**ETS prototype public speaking assessment (Joe, Kitchen, Chen & Feng 2015, ETS RR-15-36)**
- Expert raters (teaching or oral-communication evaluation backgrounds) had trouble agreeing on rubric dimensions. Low-inference dimensions were more reliable: visual aids .65, vocal expression .75. The holistic score was .63. The authors say "a significant investment in task, rubric, and training development" is needed before large-scale use — [ETS RR-15-36](https://ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2015/jvoa.html)
- Between expert and nonexpert raters, reliability ran from 0.23 to 0.71 across dimensions. Effectual persuasion and word choice were the least reliable — [AAC&U VALUE Research Hub summary of RR-15-36](https://www.aacu.org/value-research-hub/a-prototype-public-speaking-skills-assessment-an-evaluation-of-human-scoring-quality-research-report-ets-rr-15-36). Note: a search summary said this study scored speeches with the PSCR, but neither fetched page confirms which rubric was used. Treat it as "a prototype public-speaking rubric."

**Thomson & Rucker (2002), Public Speaking Competency Instrument**
- The instrument has items on volume, gestures, eye contact, appearing relaxed and comfortable, and voice and body expressiveness. It has no items for facial expression or posture. This description is secondary, from a 2021 arXiv paper on automatic presentation-competence estimation — [Sümer et al. 2021, arXiv 2105.02636](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.02636). That paper's own human ratings used a different instrument, the Tübingen Instrument for Presentation Competence (TIP), on 251 student videos — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02636)

**Job interview ratings**
- Interview interrater reliability rises with structure and with panels. Huffcutt, Culbertson & Weyhrauch (2013, *IJSA* 21:264–276), as reported in a secondary review: low structure .40 (separate interviewers) and .55 (panel), medium .48 and .73, high .61 and .78 — [Redalyc review](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/2313/231353576003/movil)
- In Huffcutt et al. (2001), applied social skills were among the most-rated interview constructs. Communication skills had the lowest mean corrected validity, .24 (secondary summary) — [Huffcutt et al. 2001 outline, PDX](https://web.pdx.edu/~mccunee/quant_621/Outlines/Huffcutt%20et%20al_%202001.doc); [Huffcutt construct meta-analysis](https://researchgate.net/profile/Allen-Huffcutt/publication/325671211_Identification_and_meta-analytic_assessment_of_psychological_constructs_measured_in_employment_interviews/links/5c72a2aa458515831f6cb513/Identification-and-Meta-Analytic-Assessment-of-Psychological-Constructs-Measured-in-Employment-Interviews.pdf)

### Inferences
- The shared core across instruments maps closely onto the app's current "clear point" judge: central message stated explicitly (VALUE's top versus bottom level is "stated" versus "can be deduced"), support (reason or example), and organization including a conclusion. Language (word choice, fit) and delivery (voice, fillers) are the other two shared families.
- Content judgments (persuasion, word choice) are where human raters disagree most, and observable things (vocal expression, visual aids) where they agree most. Friend raters will likely agree on "was the main point stated in the first sentence?" far more than on "how persuasive was it?"
- Structure helps reliability in interviews the same way anchored rubrics help in speech rating: fixed questions, anchored scales and several raters.
- Based on general knowledge, not verified in this round: the NCA form's eight competencies are usually listed as topic, thesis/purpose, supporting material, organization (preparation), and language, vocal variety, pronunciation/grammar/articulation, physical behavior (delivery). Verify against the GMU form before quoting.

### Gaps
- Actual reliability coefficients from the NCA manual (pp. 26–27), from the PSCR 2012 paper (interrater agreement, factor structure), and from Thomson & Rucker 2002 were not retrieved (paywalled or not online).
- No reliability study specific to the AAC&U Oral Communication VALUE rubric was found.
- **Which dimensions predict the overall rating most:** I found no source that reports this for any of these instruments. The ETS holistic reliability (.63) is reported, but not which dimensions drive it.
- Debate adjudication criteria (e.g., WUDC matter/manner/method) were not researched in this round.

## 2. What laypeople mean by "articulate" or "well-spoken"

### Takeaway
Hearing someone speak raises judgments of their intellect relative to reading the same words, so voice carries real signal for "competent and thoughtful." Fluency is the main cue listeners use to judge whether a speaker knows their material, and confident gestures do not offset verbal stumbles. But fluency and credibility can come apart, and the effect of pauses depends on whether the listener is judging style or content.

### Cited Findings
- Schroeder & Epley (2015, *Psychological Science*, "The Sound of Intellect"): evaluators who heard job candidates' pitches rated them more competent, thoughtful and intelligent, and were more interested in hiring them, than evaluators who read the same words. This held for professional recruiters (Experiment 4). Adding video did not change evaluations beyond hearing the voice — [Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797615572906); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/2015/june/when-job-hunting-pick-up-the-phone); [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/the-sound-of-employability-interviewers-judge-your-voice.html)
- A 2023 *Applied Psycholinguistics* study found native speakers were rated more fluent but not more credible than non-native speakers. The authors argue the consequence of disfluency depends on whether listeners attend to content or to style — [Applied Psycholinguistics, task-dependent consequences of disfluency](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/24E2657CBFAEAE3A2BC7A2493983732F/S0142716423000486a.pdf/div-class-title-task-dependent-consequences-of-disfluency-in-perception-of-native-and-non-native-speech-div.pdf)
- Press summary of a newer study: speech fluency is the main cue listeners use to judge a speaker's knowledge, and confident gestures do not mask the uncertainty that disfluencies signal. Only the press piece was seen — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/confident-gestures-fail-to-mask-the-uncertainty-signaled-by-speech-disfluencies/embed/)
- A phenomenological study found listeners respond to many features beyond fluency, and their reactions vary with the sample and context — [Pacific Scholarly Commons](https://scholarlycommons.pacific.edu/shs-all/104)
- The earlier report covers filler and pause perception, including Obama's high "uh/um" rate alongside a reputation for being articulate — `reports/Proven ways to speak articulately.md`

### Inferences
- For a fluent speaker, "articulate" in lay terms seems to be fluent delivery plus a content impression (seems to know what they're saying). The literature found here does not split those two cleanly. The clearest app-relevant lever is that listeners read stumbles as uncertainty about the content.
- The Schroeder & Epley result is about voice versus text, not about which voices sound more articulate. It supports judging from audio, not just the transcript, when friends rate clips.

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly asks laypeople what "articulate" or "well-spoken" means, for example by eliciting attributes or rating native speakers' impromptu answers on "articulate" and regressing on features.
- No manager-communication-clarity or workplace "clarity" rating study was retrieved in this round.
- A preprint claiming bilingual speakers with more disfluencies were rated higher was seen in search results but is unreviewed and was not verified, so it is excluded.

## 3. Clarity in everyday and workplace talk (interview answers)

### Takeaway
Interview research treats communication as a rated construct but shows it is hard to rate reliably without structure. Ratings become reliable mainly through structured questions, anchored scales and panels. LLM work on HR interview transcripts uses dimensions close to the app's (fluency, coherence, relevance, conciseness) and reports close agreement for GPT-4-class models, but with L2 speakers and on a dataset from one group.

### Cited Findings
- Interview reliability by structure and panel (secondary): .40/.55 (low), .48/.73 (medium), .61/.78 (high), serial/panel — [Redalyc review of Huffcutt et al. 2013](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/2313/231353576003/movil)
- HURIT dataset (IIT Kharagpur, arXiv 2504.05683): 3,890 real-world HR interview transcripts, mostly from L2 English speakers in Asia. GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-3.5 Turbo "consistently showed close agreement" with expert evaluators on fluency, coherence, tone/politeness, relevance, conciseness and grammaticality. Llama-2 13B/70B agreed less consistently — [arXiv 2504.05683](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05683v1)
- In a study of LLM judges on job-interview dialogues, GPT-4o and Llama 3.3 agreed with each other more than 85% of the time only when ties counted as agreement. Ties were 32% and 17% of their judgments — [arXiv 2502.18650](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18650)
- A University of Colorado study (663 MTurk participants) found users saw LLMs as moderately able to give formative interview feedback, but the feedback was at times irrelevant or potentially offensive — [CU Experts listing](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_507372)

### Inferences
- "Conciseness" and "relevance" appear as rated dimensions in interview-transcript work but not in the classroom rubrics, which assume a prepared speech. For 60-second answers, these may matter more than "supporting material variety."

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of manager or workplace "communication clarity" ratings with reliability figures was found.
- HURIT's numeric agreement values were not extracted (only the summary claim).

## 4. Automated and LLM scoring of spoken responses for content and organization

### Takeaway
Automated speech scoring (ETS SpeechRater) is strong on delivery and language but has never been validated for discourse organization. ETS staff say directly that it cannot assess content accuracy, relevance or organization. Recent work confirms that timing features track fluency judgments but carry no information about content: once the overall score is controlled for, they correlate *negatively* with purposefulness and logicality. LLM judges on transcripts reach moderate agreement with humans (QWK around 0.6–0.7 in the best cases). High run-to-run consistency does not mean the scores are valid. Exact-match agreement overstates quality, and LLMs show odd length effects in essays: they score short, underdeveloped essays higher than humans do.

### Cited Findings
**ETS SpeechRater**
- TOEFL Junior Speaking: human–machine r = .81 against human–human r = .89, using SpeechRater features plus a new content feature — [ETS RR-15-09](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2015/jufc.html)
- SpeechRater 5.0 (deployed 2016) has features for pronunciation, prosody, vocabulary, grammar, content and discourse, plus filters for nonscorable responses. The abstract gives no agreement figures — [ETS RR-18-10](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2018/jyzm.html)
- Content features correlate moderately highly with human proficiency scores. ASR errors lower the correlation, but word-confidence weighting brings it back close to the human-transcript level — [Xie, Evanini & Zechner 2012, NAACL](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/N12-1011.pdf)
- Hsieh & Zechner (ETS, EALTA 2019): automated scoring "cannot directly evaluate content accuracy or relevance and discourse organization" — [EALTA 2019 slides](https://ealta.eu/conference/2019/Conference%20presentations/EALTA%202019%20Powerpoints/Automated%20assessment%20of%20fluency_Hsieh%20&%20Zechner.pdf)
- SpeechRater v1.0 validity analyses — [ETS RR-08-62](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2008/hukv.html)

**Timing features vs content (ICNALE; Uehara, arXiv 2608.26137, single-author industry preprint, Aug 2026, unreviewed)**
- Data: 130 L2 English roleplay speeches (first 90 s), each rated by about 80 trained raters on 10 analytic criteria plus holistic, on a 0–10 scale.
- Human ceiling: mean pairwise inter-rater ρ = 0.393. Mean single rater versus consensus ρ = 0.57–0.62 across criteria. Cronbach's α of the 80-rater mean = 0.979.
- Fluency correlates with mean length of run +0.749, pause ratio −0.715, speech rate including pauses +0.676, long-pause rate −0.580, and pure articulation rate +0.08 (null). The 5-feature composite reaches ρ = 0.764. Text-LLM arms reach 0.687–0.774.
- Partial correlations controlling for the holistic score: Fluency +0.44, Accuracy +0.27, versus Sophistication −0.30, Purposefulness −0.21, Logicality −0.17. The authors read the composite as delivery-specific.
- Writing pause locations inline in the LLM prompt did not beat aggregate pause statistics (B−A = −0.069, 95% CI [−0.15, +0.08]). The design cannot detect effects smaller than about 0.1–0.15 ρ.
- Source: [arXiv 2608.26137 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26137v1)

**LLM scoring of speech transcripts (L2)**
- Finnish L2 speech: an LLM given ASR transcripts reaches QWK 0.67 with soft-label few-shot prompting, against 0.73 human–human — [AAAS 2025 workshop, ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.aaas-1.2.pdf)
- Zero-shot Qwen 2.5 72B on S&I Corpus transcripts with CEFR descriptors, scoring 10 analytic aspects — [arXiv 2507.10200](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10200). A review says its statistics are overclaimed and asks for confidence intervals — [Pith review](https://pith.science/paper/2507.10200)
- Speech LLMs for holistic 1–6 scoring — [arXiv 2505.21148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.21148)
- Comparative (pairwise) judgment reportedly beats pointwise scoring by a wide margin (QWK 0.633 versus 0.021 on TOEFL11 with Llama-3.1-8B, LCES). This is a secondary citation inside 2608.26137 and was not verified — [arXiv 2608.26137](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26137v1)
- ChatGPT-4 rating interpreting transcripts showed a pattern moderately similar to human raters, but the reliability of individual ratings was rather low — [Iowa State Digital Press chapter](https://www.iastatedigitalpress.com/plugins/books/154/chapter/1229)

**LLM judges: agreement, consistency, bias (text)**
- GPT-4 versus e-rater on GRE, Praxis and TOEFL writing: human–e-rater QWK was higher than human–GPT-4 by more than 0.1 for GRE and Praxis — [arXiv 2501.02334](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.02334)
- Mathew, Taher, Kundu & Barbosa (arXiv 2603.23714, Mar 2026): GPT and Llama models out of the box show "relatively weak" agreement with human essay scores. LLMs score short, underdeveloped essays higher than humans and longer essays with minor errors lower. Scores track the model's own praise or criticism — [arXiv 2603.23714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23714)
- Norman, Rivera & Hughes (arXiv 2606.19544, Jun 2026): 21 judges, 9 providers, about 541k judgments on MT-Bench, JudgeBench and RewardBench. Exact-match agreement overstates quality ("kappa deflation" of 33–41 points on MT-Bench). Test-retest reliability exceeds 0.95 for some judges, yet two production judges show severe position bias (>0.10), a "consistency–bias paradox." Verbosity bias was small (<0.011) in their single pairwise setup. Judge rankings move by up to 14 places across benchmarks — [arXiv 2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)
- Kucia et al. (ICCS 2026) compare holistic and analytic LLM essay scoring and list known vulnerabilities: position bias, verbosity bias, prompt-phrasing sensitivity — [arXiv 2604.00259](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.00259)
- Scoring bias in LLM-as-judge (score-based rather than pairwise) — [arXiv 2506.22316](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.22316v3)

### Inferences
- Plain-code metrics (pace, pauses, run length) are good proxies for listener fluency judgments, but the ICNALE result says they cannot stand in for "clear point." The judge is needed for content, and its agreement should be checked separately from delivery.
- The app's judge (Claude, guide v1, 65% mark agreement on 9 clips per ARCHITECTURE.md) is in the range the literature reports for LLM-on-transcript scoring. Report chance-corrected agreement (weighted kappa or QWK), not just percent agreement, per Norman et al.
- "Same score run to run" (already in `evals:judge`) is necessary but not sufficient. Norman et al. show consistent judges can still be biased.
- The short-essay leniency finding matters for 60-second answers: a check that the judge does not reward short, thin answers (or penalize longer, fuller ones) belongs in the evals. This is an inference from text essays, not tested on speech.
- Pairwise comparison ("which of these two answers is clearer?") may be a more reliable judge and rater format than absolute scores. Evidence is one unverified secondary figure.

### Gaps
- No study was found of Claude specifically scoring speech transcripts for organization or clarity.
- No study measured LLM run-to-run consistency on spoken-response transcripts (only text benchmarks).
- No study of native-speaker (fluent adult) impromptu speech scored by LLMs for organization was found. Everything retrieved is L2 or written essays.
- SpeechRater v5 human–machine agreement numbers were not retrieved (abstract only).

## 5. Small rater panels: how many raters and clips, scale design, training

### Takeaway
A single untrained rater is noisy. Trained raters in a large study agreed with each other at only ρ ≈ 0.39 per pair, and the reliability of a panel average grows by the Spearman-Brown formula. Report ICC(2,1) for one rater and ICC(2,k) for the panel average, read the lower confidence bound against the Koo & Li bands, use anchored low-inference items, and keep training until a target is met.

### Cited Findings
- Koo & Li (2016) ICC bands: below 0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, above 0.90 excellent. Judge by the lower bound of the 95% CI, and report the ICC form, number of subjects and raters — [Koo & Li 2016 listing](https://papers.mewayz.com/paper/oa_W2327037637); [effectsize interpret_icc docs](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/effectsize/versions/0.7.0/topics/interpret_icc)
- Use the average-measures ICC(k) only if the final score will be the mean of several raters. The average is always more reliable than one rater (Spearman-Brown). Two-way, absolute-agreement, single-rater ICC is the usual default — [MetricGate ICC(2,1)](https://metricgate.com/docs/icc-2-1-two-way-random/); [Datanovia ICC in R](https://www.datanovia.com/learn/biostatistics/inter-rater-reliability/icc-in-r)
- Low-inference dimensions are rated more reliably (vocal expression .75, visual aids .65 versus holistic .63). Persuasion and word choice are least reliable — [ETS RR-15-36](https://ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2015/jvoa.html); [AAC&U hub](https://www.aacu.org/value-research-hub/a-prototype-public-speaking-skills-assessment-an-evaluation-of-human-scoring-quality-research-report-ets-rr-15-36)
- Do not stop rater training before raters show satisfactory reliability — [Szafran 2017, PARE](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/pare/article/id/1630)
- PSCR+ (0–4 scale, 4 raters, 4 talks) reported that minimal training was needed and used rwg/awg/ADM agreement indices — [Dryad PSCR+](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.6m905qg6r)
- Interview panels are more reliable than separate interviewers at every level of structure (e.g., .61 vs .78 for high structure, secondary source) — [Redalyc review](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/2313/231353576003/movil)
- Trained-rater baseline: mean pairwise ρ = 0.393, and a single rater versus an 80-rater consensus ρ ≈ 0.57–0.62 — [arXiv 2608.26137](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26137v1)

### Inferences
- Spearman-Brown projection, my own calculation from the ICNALE pairwise ρ = 0.393: 2 raters ≈ 0.56, 3 raters ≈ 0.66, 5 raters ≈ 0.76. From the ETS holistic single-rater .63: 3 raters ≈ 0.84. So with 2–3 friends, expect "moderate" panel reliability on holistic or content judgments, and better on concrete yes/no items.
- Practical design for the app's friend panel, synthesized from the above:
  - (a) A few anchored, observable items that mirror the shared rubric core:
    - "Could you say the main point in one sentence after hearing it?" (yes/no plus write it)
    - "Was there a reason or example?"
    - "Did it end clearly?"
    - "How much effort did it take to follow?" (e.g., 1–5 with anchors)
  - (b) One short calibration round on 3–5 shared clips with discussion before real rating.
  - (c) Everyone rates the same clips (fully crossed), so ICC(2,k) applies.
  - (d) Report ICC with a CI, and the judge's weighted kappa against the panel mean, not against one person.
- On clip count, ICC CIs are very wide with few subjects. 20–30 clips is a commonly used floor for reliability studies, but no source in this round supports a specific number (see Gaps). The current 9-clip judge eval is too small to separate judges (which ARCHITECTURE.md already notes).
- A "write the main point you heard" item gives a non-numeric check. The judge's extracted main point can be compared to what friends wrote. This idea is not from a source.

### Gaps
- No source was retrieved that gives a minimum number of clips (subjects) for a stable ICC. Sample-size planning methods for ICC exist (e.g., Walter, Eliasziw & Donner 1998; Bonett 2002, from general knowledge), but they were not verified here.
- No study was found on rater training for lay or friend raters of speech clarity specifically.
- No study was found that validates a "listening effort" or "ease of following" scale for fluent native speech. That literature (listening effort in audiology and intelligibility research) is likely covered by another research thread.
