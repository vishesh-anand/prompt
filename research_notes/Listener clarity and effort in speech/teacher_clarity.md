# Teacher clarity and instructional explanation: speech behaviours that make spoken explanations clear to listeners

Scope note: builds on `reports/Proven ways to speak articulately.md`, which already covered um/uh rates, hedges, macro signposts (Chaudron & Richards), concreteness (reading), task repetition and feedback design. This file adds the teacher-clarity and instructional-explanation literature. Almost all of it is classroom lectures (15-20 minute lessons or whole courses), with listeners tested on content recall. Transfer to a 1-2 minute impromptu answer is judged item by item below.

Access caveat: the full texts of Titsworth et al. (2015), Chesebro (2003), Smith & Land (1981), Land & Smith (1979), Wittwer & Renkl (2008) and Brown & Atkins (1988) were paywalled or scanned and could not be read. Their findings below come from abstracts (ERIC) and from Fendick's (1990) dissertation, which reviews the 1960s-80s studies in detail and is fully readable on archive.org. Where only a secondary source was available, that is said.

## 1. Teacher clarity meta-analyses: how big is the effect on learning and on affect?

### Takeaway
Teacher clarity has a moderate, well-replicated link with learning: r ≈ .35 with achievement gain (Fendick 1990, the source of Hattie's d = 0.75) and about 13% of variance in learning (Titsworth et al. 2015, about r ≈ .36). The link with liking and motivation (affective learning) is larger than with test scores. All of these are "global clarity" (organization + explanation + examples + checking understanding), mostly rated by students, so they say clarity matters but say little about which single speech behaviour does the work.

### Cited Findings
- **Fendick (1990), the meta-analysis behind Hattie's figure.** Unpublished University of Florida PhD. Correlation between teacher clarity and class achievement gain = **.35 ± .05**; the meta-analytic method made no difference; the dimensions did not differ significantly from each other; a combined factor score (two or more dimensions) gave **.60 ± .13**. Effects were larger with student raters than observers (.40 vs .28), with experienced teachers, and at college level. Class size and subject made no difference. — [Fendick 1990, full text (archive.org)](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Fendick's definition: clarity of (a) organization, (b) explanation, (c) examples and guided practice, (d) assessment of student learning, with **clarity of speech as a prerequisite**. — [Fendick 1990](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- By dimension: explanation r = .35, organization .32, assessment of learning .32, examples and guided practice .23 (not significantly different). **Clarity of speech had only 4 results, "spread over a wide positive range so nothing can be said about this dimension — except that the correlation with achievement gain is positive."** — [Fendick 1990, p. 87-88](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Hattie's *Visible Learning* (2009) reports teacher clarity as **d = 0.75**, taken from Fendick's single meta-analysis (r = .35 converts to d ≈ 0.75). — [FHNW Hattie wiki](https://web.fhnw.ch/plattformen/hattie-wiki/w/images/Unter_der_Lupe_Klarheit_der_Lehrperson.pdf); [Visible Learning (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_learning). A critic reports Fendick's effect by sector as primary d = 0.54, secondary 0.63, university 0.93, and argues this may be a rater artefact (students rate at university). — [Paul Urry, Substack](https://paulurry68.substack.com/p/educational-thinking-16-john-hattie). Hattie's common-language effect sizes were computed wrongly throughout the book (his d values were not). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_learning)
- **Titsworth, Mazer, Goodboy, Bolkan & Myers (2015), Communication Education 64(4), 385-418.** Meta-analysis 1: 144 effects, N = 73,281; clarity accounts for **about 13% of variance in student learning**. Meta-analysis 2: 46 studies, N = 13,501, random effects; study design (survey vs experiment) moderated the effect on affective learning; no significant moderators for cognitive learning. Clarity has a **larger effect on affective than cognitive learning**; neither effect was homogeneous. The authors recommend that future research **return to low-inference behavioural measures** rather than high-inference perceptions. — [ERIC EJ1082982](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1082982); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279232651_Two_Meta-analyses_Exploring_the_Relationship_between_Teacher_Clarity_and_Student_Learning)
- Benton & Li (IDEA paper) summarise Titsworth et al. as finding "considerable variability in the magnitude of the correlations," attributed to the lack of a consistent, precise definition of clarity. — [Benton & Li, IDEA Paper 83](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)
- Student clarity ratings are reliable: within-class agreement on "Explained course material clearly and concisely" rwg = .84; the same instructor's clarity rating across 4+ courses r > .90. Clarity ratings correlate r = .67 with "I really wanted to take a course from this instructor" and r = .72 with "more positive feelings toward this field." — [Benton & Li, IDEA Paper 83](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)
- Mechanism evidence: clear teaching lowers listeners' **receiver apprehension** (fear of misunderstanding) (Chesebro 2003) and **extraneous cognitive load** (Bolkan, Goodboy & Myers 2017). — [Benton & Li](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf). In Bolkan, Goodboy & Kelsey (2016), 128 participants were randomly assigned to a clear or unclear video lecture; the paper models performance as a product of ability and motivation to process the material. — [ERIC EJ1094616](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1094616). A related paper reports that students in the clear lesson earned higher exam scores *because* they experienced lower cognitive load. — [Semantic Scholar, "The effect of clarity on learning: impacting motivation through cognitive load"](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-effect-of-clarity-on-learning:-impacting-load-Serki-Bolkan/86d606d0535427bbc5a2fc7b1775c8e29858a263)
- Bolkan's Clarity Indicators Scale (2017) found five dimensions of instructor clarity: **coherence, interaction, structure, disfluency, and working-memory overload**. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/304811894_Development_and_validation_of_the_clarity_indicators_scale) (via search snippet; item wording not seen)

### Inferences
- The headline numbers (d = 0.75, 13% variance) are for global clarity over whole courses. They justify "clarity matters" but should not be quoted as the effect of any one transcript feature. Fendick explicitly found too little data on clarity of speech to estimate it.
- "Larger effect on affect than on cognition" is relevant to the app: listeners' impression that a speaker is clear (and their comfort listening) moves more than their recall. That supports the owner's goal ("understood with little effort") as a perception goal, which is closer to what the clarity literature measures well.
- Bolkan's five dimensions map cleanly onto transcript features: disfluency (fillers, restarts), structure (preview/summary), coherence (links between sentences), working-memory overload (too much new information, too fast). "Interaction" does not apply to a monologue.

### Gaps
- Titsworth et al.'s exact pooled r/ρ for cognitive vs affective learning could not be read (paywalled). "13% of variance" implies r ≈ .36 overall; the separate cognitive and affective values are not in the notes.
- No meta-analysis isolates individual verbal behaviours; the closest is the Smith & Land experimental programme (section 3).

## 2. Chesebro & McCroskey's clarity measures and the Chesebro (2003) clear vs unclear lecture

### Takeaway
Chesebro's model splits clarity into **structural clarity** (preview, transitions, internal summaries, review) and **verbal clarity** (no um/uh/like, straightforward explanation, no tangents, steady pace, examples). The 2003 experiment showed that a lecture built with these behaviours produced more learning, less receiver apprehension and more liking than an unclear one; nonverbal immediacy added liking but not learning. Exact manipulations could only be confirmed from secondary sources.

### Cited Findings
- Chesebro (2003), "Effects of Teacher Clarity and Nonverbal Immediacy on Student Learning, Receiver Apprehension, and Affect," Communication Education 52(2), 135-147. Abstract: clarity was "an important factor in student learning, receiver apprehension, and affect"; nonverbal immediacy "did not have a significant effect on learning, but did increase students' affect for the instructor and the course material." — [ERIC EJ671182](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ671182); [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03634520302471). The precursor dissertation (WVU, 1999) used a similar videotaped design. — [WVU repository](https://researchrepository.wvu.edu/etd/8622)
- Behaviours attributed to Chesebro (2003), per Benton & Li: **Providing structure** by explaining objectives, **previewing the main ideas**, **linking upcoming topics with the current topic**, **summarizing frequently**, **reviewing main ideas at the end**, displaying and following an outline, skeletal notes. **Being verbally clear** by **avoiding "uh," "um," and "like,"** explaining material in a straightforward manner, **steering clear of tangents**, pacing, and **providing examples**. — [Benton & Li, IDEA Paper 83, p. 9](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)
- Chesebro & Wanzer's model: a structural element (message organization, previews, transitions, reviews) and a verbal element (examples, clear explanations, staying on task); linked across written scenarios, surveys and experiments to lower receiver apprehension, better affect, motivation, perceived and actual learning. No effect sizes on that page. — [iResearchNet, "Teacher Clarity"](https://communication.iresearchnet.com/educational-communication/teacher-clarity/)
- Teacher Clarity Short Inventory (Chesebro & McCroskey 1998, Communication Research Reports 15(3), 262-266): 10 items, 5-point Likert, covering clarity of content and of process; student-perception measure, not a behaviour count. — [Frontiers 2021 (uses TCSI)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.756165/full); [Studocu copy of TCSI](https://www.studocu.com/ph/document/university-of-southeastern-philippines/elementary-education/tcsi-development-of-the-teacher-clarity-short-inventory/144828033)
- Clarity and nonverbal immediacy are strongly correlated in student ratings (r = .64, Violanti et al. 2018 replication). — [FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1080/03634520109379232/)
- Hativa (1998), university teaching, lists "making good oral presentations": clear speech (loudness, rate, articulation, pitch variation), **simple language (familiar words, short sentences, conversational language)**, **being fluent (avoiding pauses)**, **avoiding signals of hesitation/vagueness ("um," "you know")**, and "embedding": **connecting new information to existing knowledge, micro-reviews, breaking explanations into steps**. — [Benton & Li, p. 9-10](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)
- Kennedy et al. (1978) "Clear Teacher Checklist" low-inference items include staying on topic until most students understand, **employing repetition frequently**, and pacing. — [Benton & Li, p. 9](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)

### Inferences
- The structural items that survive compression to a 60-second answer are: a one-line preview of the point, explicit links between parts ("that's why," "for example"), and a closing restatement. "Summarizing frequently" and outlines are lecture-scale and do not transfer.
- Verbal-clarity items that are transcript-detectable: filler rate (already measured), tangents (sentences unrelated to the point, which an LLM judge could flag with quoted evidence), presence of at least one concrete example, short sentences.
- Because the TCSI is a perception scale, the app cannot borrow its numbers, but its items could inform a "would a listener rate this clear?" judge rubric.

### Gaps
- Exact scripts of the 2003 clear vs unclear lectures (which behaviours, how many, effect sizes on recall) were not readable; the behaviour list is from Benton & Li's attribution, not the methods section.

## 3. Vagueness research: Hiller, Fisher & Kaess (1969), Smith & Land (1981) and later

### Takeaway
This is the best behaviour-level evidence in the clarity literature, though moderate overall: the experiments come mostly from one group (Land & Smith) with no independent replication found. Vague terms are a defined, countable word list (9 categories). In content-controlled lectures, the proportion of vague terms correlated r = -.59 and -.48 with student test scores, and Smith & Land's review of 10 experiments found vague terms lowered learning in all 10, and also made listeners rate the lesson as less effective. Caution: the lessons were 15-20 minutes, listeners were students tested on content, and the categories overlap heavily with hedges (covered in the earlier report).

### Cited Findings
- **Hiller, Fisher & Kaess (1969)**, "A computer investigation of verbal characteristics of effective classroom lecturing," American Educational Research Journal 6(4), 661-675. Frequency counts of **15-minute content-controlled lectures** in 12th-grade social studies (Stanford Explanation Project). Five factors: verbal fluency, optimal information amount, knowledge structure cues, interest, vagueness. Correlation of vagueness with student learning **r(32) = -.59** (Yugoslavia lectures, p < .005) and **r(23) = -.48** (Thailand lectures, p < .05). — [Fendick 1990, p. 15](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt); [Provalis, Hiller vagueness dictionary](https://provalisresearch.com/blog/hiller-vagueness-content-analysis-dictionary)
- Hiller et al. define vagueness as "a psychological construct which refers to the state of mind of the performer who does not sufficiently command the facts or the understanding required for maximally effective communication" (p. 670): i.e., vague words are a symptom of not knowing exactly what you mean. — [Fendick 1990, p. 20-21](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- **The vagueness categories** (Hiller et al. 1969, as quoted in Smith & Land 1981, p. 37):
  - *Ambiguous designation:* conditions, other, somehow, somewhere, someplace, thing.
  - *Approximation:* about, almost, approximately, fairly, just about, kind of, most, mostly, much, nearly, pretty (much), somewhat, sort of.
  - *"Bluffing" and recovery:* actually, and so forth, and so on, anyway, as anyone can see, as you know, basically, clearly, frankly, in a nutshell, in essence, in fact, in other words, obviously, of course, so to speak, to make a long story short, to tell the truth, you know, you see.
  - *Error admission:* excuse me, I'm sorry, I guess, I'm not sure.
  - *Indeterminate quantification:* a bunch, a couple, a few, a little, a lot, several, some, various.
  - *Multiplicity:* aspect(s), kind(s) of, sort(s) of, type(s) of.
  - *Negated intensifiers:* not all, not many, not very.
  - *Possibility:* chances are, could be, may, maybe, might, perhaps, possibly, seem(s).
  - *Probability:* frequently, generally, in general, normally, often, ordinarily, probably, sometimes, usually.
  — [Fendick 1990, p. 21-22, quoting Smith & Land 1981](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt). The later computerised version (WordStat "Communication Vagueness" dictionary) uses 10 categories. — [Provalis](https://www.tstat.it/specifiche/12041/); [Kovcomp](https://www.kovcomp.co.uk/wordstat/CommunicationVagueness.html)
- Chilcoat (1987) added to the vagueness measure **pronouns whose referent is unclear** and "I could tell you, but... of course I could" as bluffing. Fendick also lists **ambiguous pronouns** ("the teacher says 'he' and the students have no idea to whom the teacher is referring") and "continual 'uh's" as inhibitors of clarity of speech. — [Fendick 1990, p. 11, 22](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- **Smith & Land (1981)**, "Low-inference verbal behaviors related to teacher clarity," Journal of Classroom Interaction 17(1), 37-42: "high frequencies of teacher vagueness terms inhibit student achievement and cause students to perceive the lesson as ineffective." — [ERIC EJ258747](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ258747). A later article says Smith and Land reviewed **10 experimental studies; in all 10, vagueness terms negatively affected learning.** — [J. Educational Research 1985 (search snippet)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/00220671.1985.10885618)
- Hiller, Marcotte & Martin (1969): in 256 high-school essays, less vague essays received higher grades (written, not spoken). — [Provalis](https://provalisresearch.com/blog/hiller-vagueness-content-analysis-dictionary)
- Smith & Land concluded eliminating these patterns entirely is unrealistic but reducing their frequency may improve effectiveness, and they should not be the sole measure of clarity. — [Tips for Teachers summary](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/research-low-inference-verbal-behaviors-related-to-teacher-clarity/)
- Critique: vagueness terms, mazes, utterances and uncertainty terms "appear on face to be essentially the same variable." — [J. Educational Research 1985 (snippet)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/00220671.1985.10885618)
- Dunkin (1978) coded "number of vague terms by teacher" as a clarity-of-speech variable in a classroom study. — [Fendick 1990, Table A-1](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)

### Inferences
- **Overlap with the app's current filler list:** "actually," "basically," "you know" are in Hiller's *bluffing and recovery* category. So the app's choice to count them has listener-learning support from this literature, though under a different label (vagueness rather than filler).
- **New, countable categories the app does not yet measure:** *ambiguous designation* ("thing," "stuff," "somehow"), *indeterminate quantification* ("a lot," "some," "a few," "various"), *multiplicity* ("kind of," "types of," "aspects"). These are concreteness markers: they flag where a number, a name or an example was available but not given. They are plain-code detectable with a word list, which fits the "measuring is plain code" rule.
- *Possibility, probability and error admission* overlap with hedges (covered in the earlier report). Do not double-count.
- Many listed words have legitimate uses ("about 40%," "some people" in an honest estimate, "probably" for real uncertainty). Hiller's construct is about density, not presence. A rate per minute or per 100 words is the defensible measure; a per-word "vague!" flag is not. A good coaching move is pointing to one specific instance and asking "which thing?" / "how many?".
- Transfer: plausible. The mechanism (listener can't build a precise mental model from vague referents) does not depend on lecture length. But no study tested native-speaker impromptu answers, and in lecture studies the speaker's command of content was a confound (vagueness as a symptom of not knowing the facts).

### Gaps
- The per-minute rates of vague terms in the manipulated conditions and the achievement differences in each of the 10 experiments could not be read (Smith & Land 1981 full text not accessible).
- No study found that tested the categories separately (e.g., whether "things" hurts more than "maybe").

## 4. Mazes and disfluency in teacher speech (Land & Smith)

### Takeaway
"Mazes" (false starts, halts, redundantly spoken words, tangles of words) were manipulated experimentally alongside vagueness. Lessons with mazes produced lower achievement and lower clarity ratings, with a reported threshold rate of about 5.1 mazes a minute; adding extra unexplained content did *not* matter in the same experiment. Listeners reliably noticed the difference.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: mazes are "false starts or halts in speech, redundantly spoken words, and tangles of words," e.g. "will enab... will get," and "uh" (L. Smith 1977). — [Fendick 1990, p. 22](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- **Land & Smith (1979)**: a 2 (vagueness vs none) × 2 (mazes vs none) × 2 (additional unexplained content vs none) design; N = 150 education and psychology undergraduates; **20-minute videotaped lessons**; 17-item criterion-referenced test. Significant relationship with achievement for **vagueness and mazes**, but **not for extra unexplained content**. — [Fendick 1990, p. 21](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt); [Land & Smith 1979, J. Teacher Education](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002248717903000322)
- Two experiments (Smith & Land 1979a; 1980) reportedly found that **mazes at a rate of 5.1 per minute** had a negative effect on achievement and on students' perception of instructional clarity. Source: a search snippet attributed to an ERIC document (results included [ED232869](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED232869.pdf) and [ED331809](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED331809.pdf)); neither PDF could be read to confirm it. Treat as unverified.
- Land & Smith (1979), J. Educational Research 72(4), 196-197: randomized posttest-only experiment, N = 50, clear vs unclear lesson, comprehension and application test. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00220671.1979.10885153)
- **Land (1981)**: two videotaped lessons with identical content, differing only in mazes (false starts, redundant words) and utterances of "uh" and "ok". College students **rated the lesson without them as clearer**, showing listeners discriminate. — [Benton & Li, p. 3](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf). Land (1979) found students of teachers using disfluencies, tangents and vagueness scored lower on immediate recall after a videotaped lecture. — [iResearchNet](https://communication.iresearchnet.com/educational-communication/teacher-clarity/)
- **Discontinuity**: Smith & Cotten (1980) linked lesson discontinuity (and vagueness) to lower achievement; Kounin (1970) found that interjecting irrelevant content, or relevant content at the wrong time, caused loss of lesson momentum (not an achievement measure). — [iResearchNet](https://communication.iresearchnet.com/educational-communication/teacher-clarity/); [Fendick 1990, p. 21](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Hiller et al. (1969) also found higher verbal fluency went with lower vagueness and higher test scores. — [Provalis](https://provalisresearch.com/blog/hiller-vagueness-content-analysis-dictionary)

### Inferences
- Mazes ≈ the app's "restarts" metric. This literature supports counting restarts (false starts, self-repairs, repeated words) separately from um/uh, and expressing them per minute. 5.1 a minute is the only threshold anywhere in this literature; it is from manipulated lectures, so treat it as a rough "clearly too many" marker, not a target.
- Note the tension with the earlier report (Fox Tree-style evidence that um/uh can help listeners): the clarity studies bundle "uh" with false starts and tangles, so they show that a *package* of disfluency hurts, not that each um does.
- Discontinuity/tangents is the content-level analogue of a maze: a sentence that doesn't serve the point. It needs an LLM judge with quoted evidence, not plain code.
- Transfer: good for listener perception (Land 1981 was a rating task). Recall effects were from 20-minute lessons; in a 60-second answer the absolute count will be small, so rates are noisy below ~30 seconds.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for mazes alone (separated from vagueness) were not readable. The 5.1/minute figure is from search snippets of ERIC documents whose scanned text could not be extracted.

## 5. Instructional explanation research: what makes an explanation good

### Takeaway
Explanations often fail to help learners. Wittwer & Renkl (2008) explain this with a framework: good explanations are adapted to what the listener already knows, focus on concepts and principles (the "why") rather than surface steps, fit what the listener is doing, and don't replace the listener's own thinking. The earlier clarity checklists add: explain in small connected steps, link new to known, emphasise and repeat key points, and give examples. The evidence is mostly tutoring and worked-example learning; only "adapt to the listener" and "focus on the principle" plausibly transfer to an impromptu answer.

### Cited Findings
- Wittwer & Renkl (2008), "Why instructional explanations often do not work: A framework for understanding the effectiveness of instructional explanations," Educational Psychologist 43, 49-64. Research shows instructional explanations "very often" have no positive effect on learning outcomes. — [Springer reference entry (abstract/snippet)](https://link.springer.com/referenceworkentry/10.1007/978-1-4419-1428-6_1723); [Göttingen project page](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/en/research/116760.html)
- Their framework: effective explanations are **adaptive** (to the learner's prior knowledge), **focus on concepts and principles**, and **take account of the learner's ongoing cognitive activities** (three of four characteristics; the fourth was not confirmed in sources reached, see Gaps). — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254303556_Why_Instructional_Explanations_Often_Do_Not_Work_A_Framework_for_Understanding_the_Effectiveness_of_Instructional_Explanations); [Open Colleges summary](https://www.opencolleges.edu.au/blogs/articles/customized-instruction-four-characteristics-of-effective-instructional-explanations)
- Adaptation evidence: tutors who planned around students' prior knowledge produced deeper understanding and fewer false beliefs (Wittwer & Renkl and colleagues, 2010). — [Open Colleges summary](https://www.opencolleges.edu.au/blogs/articles/customized-instruction-four-characteristics-of-effective-instructional-explanations). Explanations that first state the learner's specific gap or misconception ("marked explanations," Sánchez & García-Rodicio 2013) improved retention and transfer. — [eLearning Coach](https://theelearningcoach.com/elearning_design/instructional-explanations/)
- Explanations failing because of **listener passivity** and shallow processing (Berthold & Renkl 2010). — [Bielefeld record](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/1796478); [Springer 2010 article](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10212-010-0049-y)
- Fendick's low-inference **clarity of explanation** behaviours: "explains things simply," "**repeats and stresses** directions and difficult points," "new content is introduced in **small steps and is related through ideas held in common by contiguous discourse units** (kinetic structure)," "teaches at a pace appropriate to the topic and the students." — [Fendick 1990, Table 3-1](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Cruickshank, Myers & Moenjak (1975): 1,009 students in grades 6-9 listed what their clearest teacher did; categories included **teaching things in a related step-by-step manner** and **orienting and preparing students for what follows**. — [Fendick 1990, p. 15-16](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Solomon, Bezdek & Rosenberg (1964): a "Clarity-Expressiveness vs Obscurity-Vagueness" factor (clear and understandable, **coherence**, well organized) correlated .58 with achievement gain in 24 adult classes. — [Fendick 1990, p. 14-15](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Brophy (1987): presentations are often poorly organised, "without advance organizers... underscoring the main ideas in the middle, or review and summary at the end." — [Fendick 1990, p. 22-23](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Hiller et al. (1969) identified "optimal information amount" and "knowledge structure cues" as factors of effective lecturing (alongside fluency and low vagueness). — [Fendick 1990, p. 15](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Leinhardt (2001), "Instructional explanations: A commonplace for teaching and location for contrast," Handbook of Research on Teaching (4th ed.), 333-357; a classroom-analysis framework rather than an effect study. — [Springer reference](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-1-4419-0594-9_1); [Wits thesis applying it](https://wiredspace.wits.ac.za/items/a7064a4b-4bab-47fa-a6f9-a0146a9ae68a)

### Inferences
- "Focus on concepts and principles" translates to a transcript check: does the answer state *why* (a principle or reason), not just *what happened*? The app's "reason or example" mark already sits here.
- "Kinetic structure" (each unit linked to the next by shared ideas) is a coherence check: consecutive sentences share a referent or are joined by a link word ("because," "so," "which means"). An LLM judge could quote the jump where a new idea arrived unconnected. Plain code could approximate it with connective counts, but that is weak.
- "Adapt to the listener's knowledge" is hard to detect without knowing the listener. A practical proxy: unexplained jargon or acronyms in an Everyday-conversation answer.
- "Optimal information amount" and Land & Smith's null result for *extra unexplained content* suggest that piling on detail isn't what hurts listeners; vagueness and disfluency do. Cautious reading: one null result.

### Gaps
- Wittwer & Renkl's fourth characteristic: commonly summarised (from memory, unverified) as "explanations should not replace the learner's own knowledge-construction activities." Not confirmed in any source reached.
- Brown & Atkins (1988, *Effective Teaching in Higher Education*) and Brown & Armstrong (1984, "Explaining and explanations") could not be reached. Their "keys" of explaining and any lecture-rating data are not in these notes.
- Wittwer & Renkl (2010) meta-analysis of explanations in example-based learning: I recall a small overall effect with larger gains for conceptual than procedural knowledge, but could not open the paper to confirm the numbers, so none are given. — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225335923_How_Effective_are_Instructional_Explanations_in_Example-Based_Learning_A_Meta-Analytic_Review)

## 6. Concreteness and examples; "rule-example-rule"

### Takeaway
Examples are a standard part of every clarity definition (Fendick, Chesebro, Hativa), but the clarity literature gives little isolated evidence for them: in Fendick's meta-analysis, the "examples and guided practice" dimension had the *lowest* correlation with achievement (.23, not significantly lower than the others), and that dimension mostly means worked homework examples, not examples in speech. No primary source on "rule-example-rule" was reached.

### Cited Findings
- Examples and guided practice: r = .23 with achievement gain (lowest of the four dimensions, not significantly different from explanation at .35). Its behaviours are mostly classroom practice: "shows students examples of how to do classwork," answers questions, gives individual help. — [Fendick 1990, p. 88 and Table 3-1](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Chesebro (2003) lists "providing examples" under verbal clarity. — [Benton & Li](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)
- Clear teachers provide "concrete examples that relate to students' lives and connect with past topics" (information-processing account; Benton & Li's synthesis, not a single study). — [Benton & Li, p. 4](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)
- Instructional explanations given before worked examples helped learners understand concepts but not necessarily apply them. — [PMC 2021 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8379662/)
- Bolkan & Goodboy studied example order ("Instruction, example order, and student learning: reducing extraneous cognitive load by providing structure for elaborated examples"). — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Instruction,-example-order,-and-student-learning:-Bolkan-Goodboy/119698ad8e44382c9353d0aa45d53ec9352a138d) (title only; findings not read)

### Inferences
- The best transcript-level concreteness signal from this literature is the *absence* of vague terms (section 3: "thing," "some," "a lot," "kind of"), which has stronger evidence than "has an example."
- "Rule-example-rule" is the same shape as PREP (point, reason/example, point) already in the app's rubric. Its evidence base in speech remains practitioner advice, consistent with the earlier report.

### Gaps
- No primary study of rule-example-rule (Brown & Armstrong; Rosenshine) was retrieved. I found no listener-recall experiment that isolates "one concrete example vs none" in a short spoken explanation.

## 7. Repetition and redundancy for listeners

### Takeaway
Clarity checklists consistently list repeating and stressing key points (Kennedy et al. 1978; Fendick's "repeats and stresses... difficult points"), and repeated hearing raises recall. But I found no study that sets a helpful amount of restatement in a short spoken answer, or that directly compares spoken with written redundancy. Meanwhile "redundantly spoken words" count as mazes, so repetition helps only as deliberate restatement of the point, not as stumbling.

### Cited Findings
- "Employing repetition frequently" is a Clear Teacher Checklist item (Kennedy et al. 1978). — [Benton & Li, p. 9](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)
- "Repeats and stresses directions and difficult points" is a clarity-of-explanation behaviour; "reviews the lesson at the end" is a clarity-of-organization behaviour. — [Fendick 1990, Table 3-1](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)
- Students who viewed a lecture one, two or three times recalled more at all levels of importance with more viewings (Kim et al.). This is repetition of the whole lecture, not restatement within it. — [Kim et al., "Effects of Repetition on Recall and Note-Taking"](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Sung-Il-Kim-4/publication/232541710_Effects_of_Repetition_on_Recall_and_Note-Taking_Strategies_for_Learning_From_Lectures/links/00b4952d4927b56cba000000/Effects-of-Repetition-on-Recall-and-Note-Taking-Strategies-for-Learning-From-Lectures.pdf)
- In multimedia learning, on-screen text identical to narration hurt recall (redundancy effect), while slightly mismatched text helped (Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013). That is a different kind of redundancy. — [Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013 (PDF)](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/YueBjorkBjork2013_redundancy.pdf)
- "Redundantly spoken words" are part of the maze definition that lowered achievement. — [Fendick 1990, p. 22](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)

### Inferences
- For a 60-second answer, the defensible form of repetition is one restatement of the main point at the end (the "ending" mark already in the app). Detectable: semantic overlap between the first-stated point and the last sentence (LLM judge with quotes).
- Word-level repetition ("I, I think the, the main") is a maze and should keep counting against the speaker.

### Gaps
- I found no study on the optimal amount of within-talk restatement for listeners, nor a spoken-vs-written redundancy comparison. The common claim that "listeners need more repetition than readers" was not supported by any source I could reach.
