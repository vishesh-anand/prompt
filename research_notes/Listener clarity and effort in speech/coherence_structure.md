# Coherence and structure in spoken monologues: how they are defined, measured and judged

Scope note: research round of about 20 tool calls (searches plus fetches). Primary texts read directly: Wright, Capilouto & Koutsoftas 2013 (full text, PMC), Wright et al. 2014 (full text, author manuscript), McCroskey & Mehrley (full text on the author's site), Lorch, Chen & Lemarié 2012 (abstract), Wang et al. ACL 2017 (abstract), Wang et al. NAACL 2013 (abstract), Zhao et al. 2026 (arXiv abstract). Everything else came from search summaries or secondary sources and is marked as such. Strength labels: **Strong** (replicated or large primary study), **Moderate** (single decent primary study), **Weak** (secondary, tiny n, industry, unreviewed, or only seen as a snippet).

## (a) How researchers rate global and local coherence in spontaneous spoken discourse, and how reliable the ratings are

### Takeaway
Clinical discourse research rates **global coherence** (does each utterance stay on the overall topic?) and **local coherence** (does each utterance connect to the one before?) on 3- to 5-point scales per utterance. Reported percent agreement is high (80 to 98%), but the one study that also reported Cohen's kappa found only **fair** agreement (κ = 0.36 inter-rater, 0.40 intra-rater). Healthy adults score near the ceiling on picture and story tasks, so these scales barely separate fluent speakers. Open-ended **recounts**, which resemble the app's "tell me about a time..." questions, are the one task where healthy adults lose coherence.

### Cited Findings

**Definitions**
- Global coherence is how units of discourse (utterance, proposition, sentence) maintain the overall topic. Local coherence is how the content of one unit relates to the preceding unit. The distinction goes back to Agar & Hobbs (1982), Kintsch & van Dijk (1978) and Glosser & Deser (1992). Wright et al. frame discourse organization as realized through global coherence. — [Wright, Koutsoftas, Capilouto & Fergadiotis 2014, Aging, Neuropsychology, and Cognition (author manuscript)](https://talkbank.org/aphasia/discourse/lit/Wright2014.pdf)
- Coherence is framed as the *listener's* ability to interpret the overall meaning the speaker conveys. — [Wright et al. 2014](https://talkbank.org/aphasia/discourse/lit/Wright2014.pdf)

**The scales**
- **Glosser & Deser (1990/1992):** a 5-point rating per utterance, from 5 (substantive information directly related to the topic) to 1 (incoherent). Seen here only through Wright 2013's account. — [Wright, Capilouto & Koutsoftas 2013, IJLCD 48(3):249–256](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984)
- **Van Leer & Turkstra (1999):** adapted the 5-point scale and collapsed it into low (1–2), medium (3) and high (4–5), because raters rarely gave 2 or 4. **Rogalski et al. (2010)** used only 5, 3 and 1. Both seen secondhand. — [Wright et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984)
- **Wright et al. 4-point scale:** 4 = overtly related, with details of significant importance; 3 = related, but suppositional or tangential; 2 = remotely related; 1 = entirely unrelated. Each C-unit (an independent clause plus its modifiers) gets a score, and the scores are averaged. — [Wright et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984)
- Other approaches counted the frequency and type of coherence violations (Christiansen 1995) or global coherence errors (Marini et al. 2005). Wright 2013 says Marini's method is closer to story structure than to thematic unity. — [Wright et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984)

**Reliability (the key twist)**
- **Wright 2013** (n = 50 healthy adults, ages 28–58, two wordless picture-book stories): inter-rater agreement 95.78% (5-point) and 98.19% (4-point); intra-rater 97.91% and 97.45%. Test–retest and internal consistency were *not* calculated. Between-story correlation was r = 0.66 (5-point) and r = 0.71 (4-point), and the two scales correlated r = 0.66–0.79. — [Wright et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984) (**Moderate**)
- **Wright 2014** (n = 80 healthy adults: 40 aged 20–39 and 40 aged 70–87; five tasks):
  - Percent agreement on the 4-point scale was 87.04% inter-rater and 80.53% intra-rater.
  - **Cohen's kappa on the same data was only κ = 0.36 (95% CI 0.31–0.40), "fair," inter-rater, and κ = 0.40 (CI 0.37–0.44) intra-rater.**
  - The authors say kappa had not been used in earlier global-coherence studies.
  - C-unit segmentation agreement, a step every per-utterance scale depends on, was 84.34% inter-rater and 90.94% intra-rater.
  - Raters were trained until they reached 100% agreement on practice transcripts.
  - — [Wright et al. 2014](https://talkbank.org/aphasia/discourse/lit/Wright2014.pdf) (**Moderate**; the most informative reliability figure found)
- Earlier percent-agreement figures: Glosser & Deser (1992) 78–98% across all their measures; Rogalski et al. (2010) 85.09% inter-rater and 88.49% intra-rater. — [Wright et al. 2014](https://talkbank.org/aphasia/discourse/lit/Wright2014.pdf) (secondhand)
- Wright 2013 states that the 5-point scale's reliability and validity "have not been estimated and cannot be assumed across different populations." It also says that changes to scoring across studies make results hard to compare, and suggests reconsidering the 5-level structure. — [Wright et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984)

**Use with healthy adults, and the ceiling effect**
- Yes, healthy adults are used, but mainly as controls. Glosser & Deser's original work compared fluent aphasia, dementia and traumatic brain injury against healthy controls, using family and work topics. Fluent aphasia did *not* differ from controls on global coherence; dementia and head injury did. — [summary via PMC search results / Wright 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984) (secondhand)
- Healthy middle-aged means were **4.81 of 5** (SD 0.25–0.30) and **3.93 of 4** (SD 0.09–0.13) on story tasks, which is near the ceiling. — [Wright et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984)
- Glosser & Deser (1992): middle-aged (mean age 51.9) against elderly (76.2) global coherence means were 4.28 against 3.69, with 5.1% against 17.9% of utterances incoherent. — reported in [Wright et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799984) (secondhand)
- Of the five tasks (single-picture eventcasts, sequential-picture eventcasts, stories, recounts, procedures), **recounts had the lowest coherence**. Recounts were also the only task where older adults scored significantly lower than younger ones (p < .01; no difference on the others, p = .17–.66). Memory and attention predicted coherence only in the older group. — [Wright et al. 2014](https://talkbank.org/aphasia/discourse/lit/Wright2014.pdf)
- A 2024 study in *Language Testing in Asia* applied the 4-point Global Coherence Rating Scale to structured and unstructured discourse in neurotypical adults. Its findings were not read. — [Language Testing in Asia 2024, table](https://languagetestingasia.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40468-024-00289-x/tables/1) (**Weak**: title and table only)

**Macrostructure and story grammar**
- The Multilingual Assessment Instrument for Narratives (MAIN) scores macrostructure as story-grammar elements (goal, attempt, outcome and so on) in picture-based narratives. Human–human agreement in one 2026 Mandarin study was κ = .872. — [Zhao et al. 2026, arXiv:2605.17205](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.17205) (**Weak**: unreviewed preprint)
- Rutter (1979) had students put shuffled sentences back in order. Passages of schizophrenic speech were harder to reconstruct. The abnormality lay in how sentences relate to each other, not in single sentences, which makes this a listener-side test of local coherence. — [search summary, British Journal of Psychiatry](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/positive-symptoms-and-the-organization-within-and-between-ideas-in-schizophrenic-speech/859161695F255840A5BF92E80D7713E7) (**Weak**: seen only in a snippet; clinical)

### Inferences
- Per-utterance global coherence scales were built to find impairment. For fluent healthy adults they sit at the ceiling, with very little variance, so they would not show day-to-day progress in the app's users. They measure "did you go off topic," not "was it well organized."
- High percent agreement on these scales is probably inflated by the ceiling. When almost every utterance gets a 4, raters agree by default. Kappa, which corrects for chance agreement, was only fair. Any LLM or plain-code "on topic / aside" label will face the same base-rate problem, and the app's own evals should report kappa, not percent agreement.
- Segmentation is a hidden source of error. Even trained humans agreed only 84% on where C-units split. The app's step-mapping ("point, reason, example, aside...") depends on segmenting first, so disagreements will start there.
- Recounts, the personal "tell me about a time" format, are the hardest task for healthy adults. That is plausibly where the app's Work and Everyday questions will show the most room to improve.

### Gaps
- **Ellis et al.** on coherence: not found. A search for an Ellis, Henderson, Wright & Rogalski 2016 review returned nothing, so the citation may be misremembered.
- **Van Leer & Turkstra (1999)** primary paper not read. Its reliability figures and population (thought to be TBI) are unverified.
- No study found that rates *local* coherence on a scale with kappa reported.
- No test–retest reliability for any global-coherence scale was found.
- No coherence norms exist for 1–2 minute impromptu *opinion or work* answers by healthy fluent adults. The tasks are picture descriptions, stories, recounts and procedures.
- **Topic maintenance measures** beyond global coherence (for example, topic-shift counts) were not found in this round.

## (b) Evidence that organization and coherence change listener comprehension, recall and ratings of the speaker

### Takeaway
A cluster of 1950s–60s speech-communication experiments, mostly with students listening to recorded speeches, found that a **badly scrambled** speech lowers attitude change and the speaker's perceived **authority**, and probably recall. Minor disorganization often had no effect. In the best primary study, **non-fluency hurt credibility more broadly than disorganization did**. No meta-analysis of message organization was found. Note: the "disorganized" condition in these studies is a **random shuffle**, not a late main point.

### Cited Findings
- **McCroskey & Mehrley** (commonly cited as *Speech Monographs* 36, 1969; the venue is not shown on the page read):
  - Design: 2 × 2 × 2, with organized vs disorganized message, fluent vs nonfluent delivery, and high vs low initial credibility.
  - Sample: n = 352 Michigan State public-speaking students (fall 1967), 43–46 per cell, plus a control group of 31.
  - Manipulations: the disorganized version **shuffled sentences within each section** of a speech on a guaranteed annual wage, taken from Thompson's materials. Nonfluency was a vocalized pause or repetition about every 14th word.
  - Attitudes: disorganization *or* heavy nonfluency each significantly reduced attitude change. Combining them reduced it no further.
  - Credibility: **fluency affected all three dimensions** (authoritativeness, character, dynamism). **Organization affected only authoritativeness.**
  - Manipulation check: the disorganized and nonfluent speeches were rated more "confused."
  - The authors suggest clarity may act as a "permissive" threshold variable, not a linear one, and note that only extremes were tested.
  - The page notes that Table 1 as reproduced appears out of order with the text, so check the original before citing cell means.
  - — [McCroskey & Mehrley, author's site](http://www.jamescmccroskey.com/publications/39.htm) (**Moderate**: decent n, written decades ago, students, a single recorded speech)
- Prior studies, as summarized by McCroskey & Mehrley:
  - **Smith (1951):** major disorganization reduced attitude change; minor disorganization did not.
  - **Thistlethwaite et al. (1955):** transitional material had no significant effect on attitudes.
  - **Thompson (1960):** no attitude difference between organized and randomized speeches, measured on a single scale.
  - **Sharp & McClung (1966):** disorganization lowered the credibility of an initially highly credible source.
  - **Baker (1965):** disorganization cues did not significantly change attitudes; the organized speaker *gained* credibility and the disorganized one did not.
  - Their combined reading: disorganization may lower a high-credibility source, and organization may raise a moderate one.
  - — [McCroskey & Mehrley](http://www.jamescmccroskey.com/publications/39.htm) (secondhand for each study)
- **Thompson (1960)**, *Southern Speech Journal* 26:59–69: textbooks summarize it as showing that poorly organized speeches are recalled less well. That is a different outcome from the attitude null that McCroskey reports. — [Stand Up, Speak Out, ch. 10 (open textbook)](https://open.lib.umn.edu/publicspeaking/part/chapter-10-creating-the-body-of-a-speech) (**Weak**: textbook summary)
- **Baker (1965)** was published in *Western Speech* 29:148–161, not *Speech Monographs*. Its "disorganization cues" were verbal or nonverbal signs of difficulty, such as apologizing for skipping a point or silent pauses, so they were confounded with delivery. **Sharp & McClung (1966)**, "Effects of organization on the speaker's ethos," *Speech Monographs* 33(2):182–183, is a two-page report seen only in reference lists. — [Speaking Confidently references](https://pressbooks.pub/speakingconfidently/back-matter/references/); [Saylor public speaking text](https://learn.saylor.org/mod/book/view.php?id=82548) (**Weak**: secondary)
- **Signposting in lectures:** big-picture "macro" markers ("the first point is") helped L2 listeners recall more than micro markers ("so," "well") (Chaudron & Richards 1986). This was found in the prior round and is reported there. — see the prior report, `reports/Proven ways to speak articulately.md` (**Moderate**, L2)
- **Previews in audio:** preview sentences produced much better outlines than no signals, in both print and text-to-speech audio. Headings helped in print but **not** in audio until their missing cues were put back in the spoken version. Participants were college students and the material was expository text. — [Lorch, Chen & Lemarié 2012, J. Exp. Psych.: Applied 18(3):265–276](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/communicating-headings-and-preview-sentences-in-text-and-speech/) (**Moderate**: the only spoken-modality signaling experiment found, synthetic voice)
- **Signaling reviews (written):** virtually all signal types (titles, headings, previews, summaries) improve memory for the information they cue, while memory for un-cued information is often unchanged. Signaling matters more when the topic structure is complex; Lorch & Lorch found no effect with simple structure. — [search summaries of Lorch reviews](https://scholars.uky.edu/es/publications/effects-of-organizational-signals-on-free-recall-of-expository-te/); [Lorch 1989, Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01320135) (**Moderate–Strong** for writing; not read directly)
- **Explicit conclusions:** a meta-analysis cited in a 1991 Michigan State thesis found that persuasive messages with **explicit conclusions** produce slightly more attitude change than implicit ones. The thesis models the chain as conclusion → comprehension → perceived source position → attitude. — [MSU thesis](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/22780/FULL_TEXT/view) (**Weak** here: the meta-analysis was not identified by name. It is likely O'Keefe's work on conclusion explicitness, but that is not verified.)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports "**don't be scrambled**." A random-order speech costs comprehension, persuasion and perceived authority. The effect may be a threshold (major disorganization hurts, minor does not), so a fluent speaker who is already roughly organized may gain less from structure coaching than the advice suggests.
- In the best study, **fluency (vocalized pauses, repetitions) drove credibility more broadly than organization did**. That is a reason to keep the app's filler and restart measures next to the structure mapping, and not to drop them.
- Because "disorganized" in every classic study meant *shuffled*, the classic literature says nothing about *point-first vs point-late*. That gap carries straight into (c).
- Explicit conclusions and macro signposts both have some support, which favors the "proper ending" mark and verbal signposts as things to coach.

### Gaps
- **Darnell (1963)** and **Spicer & Bassett (1976)**: not found in any search. Their findings are unverified, and nothing should be claimed about them.
- **Weaver (1969, Iowa State)**, on verbal cueing (previews, signposts, summaries) and perceived organization, retention and credibility: only the abstract metadata was seen, with no findings.
- **No meta-analysis of message organization** effects on comprehension or credibility was found.
- No modern (post-2000) replication with spontaneous, non-scripted speech, or with healthy adults judging peers' impromptu answers, was found.
- No study links *rated* global coherence (the clinical scales in (a)) to listener comprehension in healthy speakers.

## (c) Order effects: main point early vs late, deductive vs inductive order

### Takeaway
No experiment was found that tests main-point-first against main-point-late **in speech**. The written and broadcast evidence is thin and mixed. One study found that deductive vs inductive order did not change comprehension but did change opinions, depending on culture. Inverted-pyramid news did not reliably beat chronological or narrative order, and sometimes lost. Advance previews in audio do help outlining. That is the closest spoken evidence, and it concerns announcing topics, not stating the conclusion.

### Cited Findings
- **Deductive vs inductive product descriptions (written):** culturally preferred structure did **not** affect comprehension but might affect opinions. Chinese readers rated the inductive version more readable and persuasive; Western readers rated both about equally. — [Journal of Business and Technical Communication, U. Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/inductively-versus-deductively-structured-product-descriptions-ef/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1050651910380372) (**Moderate**, written; the year is listed variously)
- **Inverted pyramid vs chronological (written news):** chronological stories took more cognitive effort, but recall and enjoyment did not differ. Comprehension showed only a *marginal* edge for the inverted pyramid. — [Sternadori 2008, U. Missouri dissertation](https://mospace.umsystem.edu/items/4f5c3c35-b2a9-4339-bc2f-4672afe32f88) (**Weak–Moderate**: dissertation)
- **TV news (n = 60):** the inverted pyramid was the *least* effective structure for gaining information; chronological narrative improved recall and comprehension. — [Benaissa Pedriza, Comunicación](https://revistascientificas.us.es/index.php/Comunicacion/article/download/21257/18729/85535) (**Weak–Moderate**: audiovisual, small n)
- **"Ways With Words" (ASNE/Poynter):** reporters wrote each story four ways. The inverted pyramid scored lowest on understanding; feature or narrative formats tended to do better. — [Wylie Communications summary](https://wyliecomm.com/2020/08/inverted-pyramid-structure-of-news-writing) (**Weak**: industry study, secondhand)
- **Previews (audio and print):** preview sentences raised outline quality in text-to-speech audio. — [Lorch, Chen & Lemarié 2012](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/communicating-headings-and-preview-sentences-in-text-and-speech/) (**Moderate**)
- **Topic-introduction reading times (written):** readers read faster when an introductory paragraph previewed the topic structure and when each new topic related directly to the previous one. — [Lorch, Lorch & Matthews, "On-line processing of the topic structure of a text"](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/on-line-processing-of-the-topic-structure-of-a-text/) (**Moderate**, written)
- **Kieras (1979), "Initial Mention as a Cue to the Main Idea and the Main Item of a Technical Passage":** the abstract notes that information near the start of a passage is often recalled better, usually put down to the most important information coming first. The full report could not be read (HTTP 403), so its results are unknown. — [DTIC ADA073773](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA073773.pdf)
- **Primacy and recency across two messages (tangential):** high elaboration favors the first message (primacy) and low elaboration favors the second (recency). This concerns the order of two competing messages, not where the point sits within one answer. — [Haugtvedt & Wegener 1994, J. Consumer Research](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v21y1994i1p205-18.html) (**Moderate**, but off-target)
- **Deductive belief structure and persuasion transfer:** deductive belief structure produced more transfer of persuasion to non-targeted scenarios than inductive. — [U. Illinois dissertation 1997](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/83490) (**Weak**: dissertation, about belief structure rather than message order)

### Inferences
- "Say your point first" remains **untested in speech**, as the previous round found. This round adds that in *writing and news* the direct comparisons do **not** show a clear advantage for answer-first: comprehension is about equal, and narrative order sometimes wins on recall. The case for point-first rests on (1) listeners not being able to re-read, (2) previews and macro signposts helping listeners in audio, and (3) explicit conclusions persuading slightly more. All three are indirect.
- The likely mechanism is **giving the listener a frame early** (a preview or a stated point), not deduction as such. An early one-line frame plus a clear ending may matter more than strict deductive order. A "seconds until main point" metric is reasonable as a behavioral target, but the app should not claim it is proven to improve comprehension.
- The cultural result (inductive preferred by Chinese readers) is a caution against presenting point-first as universally better for every listener.
- The app could produce the missing evidence: compare listener or judge ratings of retries that moved the point earlier against retries that did not.

### Gaps
- **No spoken experiment** comparing main point early vs late (deductive vs inductive) was found.
- **Plain-language or BLUF studies** that test "bottom line up front" in government or business writing against a control were not found in this round.
- Kieras (1978/1979) results could not be read.
- No study measures listeners' "time to grasp the point" or listening effort as a function of where the point is placed.

## (d) Discourse features in automated spoken scoring (ETS) and agreement with humans

### Takeaway
ETS researchers (Wang, Evanini, Zechner and colleagues) annotated coherence in non-native TOEFL-style spontaneous responses and showed that coherence features modestly improve automated holistic scores. Discourse-structure (RST) annotations of speech can reach good human agreement, but automatic features correlate only moderately with expert coherence ratings (best r ≈ 0.61). All of this work uses **L2 test-takers' 45–60 s responses**, not fluent native speakers.

### Cited Findings
- **Wang, Evanini & Zechner 2013 (NAACL):** built a coherence-annotated corpus of non-native spontaneous responses and adapted essay-coherence features. Adding them improved agreement between automated and human holistic scores by **about 10% relative**. Automatic prediction of coherence scores reached a **weighted F of 73%**. — [ACL Anthology N13-1101](https://aclanthology.org/N13-1101/); [abstract mirror](https://mirror.aclweb.org/hlt-naacl2013/abstracts/274.aspx.html) (**Moderate**)
  - *Unverified:* a search snippet attributed to this line of work a 3-point coherence scale with inter-annotator **κ = 0.68** and a correlation with holistic proficiency of **r = 0.66**. The full text could not be read to confirm. — [N13-1101 PDF](https://aclanthology.org/N13-1101.pdf)
- **Wang et al. 2017 (ACL short):** RST annotation of non-native spoken responses from an academic English test. Inter-annotator kappa was **0.848 for span, 0.766 for nuclearity and 0.653 for relation**. The best discourse-structure feature correlated **0.612** with expert human coherence ratings. — [ACL Anthology P17-2041](https://aclanthology.org/P17-2041/) (**Moderate**)
- **Wang et al. 2017 (SLaTE):** a follow-up coherence-annotated corpus; it frames coherence as a key rubric criterion with little automated coverage. — [ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/slate_2017/wang17c_slate.html)
- **2019 (DISRPT workshop):** trained automatic RST parsers on these spoken annotations. — [ACL Anthology W19-2719](https://aclanthology.org/W19-2719/)
- ETS publication pages tag this work "SpeechRater," but no source read here confirms that **production** SpeechRater uses coherence features. — [ETS record](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/chapter/2013/jria.html)

### Inferences
- Coherence is measurable in spontaneous speech with moderate machine–human agreement, but its added predictive value over fluency and pronunciation features is modest (about 10% relative). For a fluent native speaker, where delivery features vary less, coherence may carry relatively more of the signal. That is untested.
- RST "nuclearity" (which segment is central and which supports it) is the formal version of the app's "point vs reason vs aside" labels. Human agreement on it was about 0.77 κ on L2 speech. That is a realistic ceiling for the app's step labels.

### Gaps
- Full texts of the ETS papers (dataset sizes, rubric wording) were not read.
- No ETS or academic work found on coherence scoring for native or fluent speakers' spontaneous speech.

## (e) LLMs rating or segmenting discourse structure, and agreement with human annotators

### Takeaway
The direct evidence is thin. On spoken narrative **macrostructure** (story grammar, Mandarin), the best LLM reached κ = .79 against a human–human κ of .87 in a 2026 preprint, and it was weaker on young adults' more complex narratives. On written summaries, LLM coherence judgments correlate only moderately with humans (Spearman ≈ 0.51–0.58). Fine-tuned LLMs now lead RST-parsing benchmarks; general-purpose zero-shot LLMs lag both supervised parsers and human agreement.

### Cited Findings
- **Zhao et al. 2026 (arXiv:2605.17205):** four LLMs annotated MAIN story-grammar macrostructure in spoken Mandarin narratives from children, young adults and older adults.
  - The best model reached **κ = .794** with humans, against **κ = .872** between humans, and cut annotation time by 65%.
  - A lightweight local model did substantially worse.
  - Reliability dropped on **young adult** narratives (richer vocabulary, ambiguity, several story elements in one utterance).
  - Fine semantic categories stayed hard.
  - — [alphaXiv abstract](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.17205); [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17205) (**Weak–Moderate**: unreviewed preprint, Mandarin, narrative rather than coherence)
- **G-Eval (GPT-4) on SummEval:** coherence Spearman ρ ≈ **0.58** with human ratings (strongest of four dimensions; average 0.51). A reimplementation got about 0.51 on coherence, so results depend on prompt and parser details. — [Microsoft AI playbook on G-Eval](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/ai/playbook/technology-guidance/generative-ai/working-with-llms/evaluation/g-eval-metric-for-summarization); [Check-Eval, arXiv 2407.14467](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.14467) (**Moderate**, written summaries)
- **Clinical dialogues (French OSCE):** human inter-rater reliability for coherence was about zero (ICC −0.040), yet LLM coherence scores correlated r = 0.76 with the human mean. That rests on only 12 dialogues. — [arXiv 2606.28526](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.28526) (**Weak**: n = 12, dialogue)
- **Open-domain dialogue:** expert coherence ratings had a leave-one-out Spearman of 0.70 between annotators, which sets the human ceiling an LLM judge can be compared against. — [arXiv 2407.03841](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.03841) (**Moderate**, dialogue)
- **RST parsing with LLMs:**
  - Fine-tuned Llama 2 70B (bottom-up) set state-of-the-art results on RST-DT, Instr-DT and GUM. — [Maekawa et al., EACL 2024](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2024.eacl-long.171/)
  - Zero-shot Llama-3.3-70B reached 70.4 span F1 against 79.8 for a supervised parser. — [Disco-RAG, arXiv 2601.04377](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04377)
  - (**Moderate**, written text)
- **BeDiscovER benchmark (2025):** supervised parsers approach human agreement on dialogue discourse parsing (STAC), general-purpose LLMs fall noticeably behind, and human agreement on full STAC structure is only moderate (κ ≈ 0.58). — [arXiv 2511.13095](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.13095) (**Moderate**)
- **Argument mining:** fine-tuned open-weight LLMs reach state-of-the-art results across argument-mining subtasks. No comparison with human agreement was found. — [Cabessa, Hernault & Mushtaq 2025, via ACL Anthology author page](https://aclanthology.org/people/hugo-hernault/) (**Weak** for this question)

### Inferences
- An LLM labelling each segment of a transcript as point, reason, example, aside, repeat or ending is closest to the MAIN macrostructure and RST nuclearity tasks. On that evidence, expect agreement roughly in the κ 0.6–0.8 range with a careful prompt and a strong model, and lower on the complex, ambiguous utterances of articulate adults. The app should check its labeller against hand labels with kappa, as the project's judge evals already do.
- Coherence as one *holistic* score is where both humans and LLMs are least reliable (ρ ≈ 0.5–0.7). Concrete, countable outputs (time to first statement of the point, whether an ending exists, number of asides) are likely more stable than a coherence grade. That matches the project's rule to count anything that can be counted.
