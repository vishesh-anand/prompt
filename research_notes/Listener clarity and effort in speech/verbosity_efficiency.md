# Rambling, circling and wordiness in spoken answers: measures, listener judgements, and when extra words help

Scope note: an earlier round (`reports/Proven ways to speak articulately.md`) covered structure, fillers, pauses, repetition and feedback, but not verbosity or informational efficiency. These notes fill that gap. Almost all of the measurement work comes from **ageing research** (off-target verbosity) or **aphasia research** (Correct Information Units). Very little studies healthy, fluent younger speakers who are "too wordy", so most transfer to the app is inference and is labelled that way.

Method note: 20+ searches and fetches. Full text read: Engelhardt et al. (2006), Hoffman et al. (2018), Bluck et al. (2016), Yin & Peng (2016). Abstracts only (via Europe PMC): James et al. (1998), Trunk & Abrams (2009), Pushkar et al. (2000), Arbuckle et al. (2000), Arbuckle & Gold (1993). Known only from search summaries or from other papers' descriptions: Davies & Katsos (2013), Nicholas & Brookshire (1993/1995), Oelschlaeger & Thorne (1999), Chaudron & Richards (1986), Titsworth & Kiewra (2004), the 2025–2026 ML/LLM CIU papers, Rubio-Fernández (2016), Arts et al. (2011). Each is flagged where cited.

## 1. Off-target verbosity (OTV): how it was measured, and how listeners rated it

### Takeaway
OTV is coded by hand. It counts how often and how far an answer strays from the question, or how many words are relevant versus irrelevant. How listeners judge it **depends on the task**. In personal stories, older adults' more off-topic narratives were rated as *better* stories (James et al. 1998), and young and old speakers were seen as equally focused, coherent and clear (Trunk & Abrams 2009). On **procedural / "how to" topics**, the more off-topic speakers were seen as **less focused, less clear and more talkative**. Conversation partners of high-OTV speakers were less satisfied (Pushkar et al. 2000), and high-OTV speakers were less efficient at getting information across (Arbuckle et al. 2000). The material that hurts is irrelevant material, not material that is indirectly relevant.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition used in the founding work:** verbose speech is speech "that was off target when replying to a stimulus in the interview", meaning it conveyed information irrelevant to the question (Gold, Andres, Arbuckle & Schwartzman, 1988, as quoted in Bluck et al. 2016). — [Bluck, Alea, Baron-Lee & Davis 2016, Psychology and Aging](https://lifestorylab.psych.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/story-asides.pdf)
- **Arbuckle & Gold scoring, two numbers per answer:** *item verbosity* (how many answers or items contain off-target material) and *extent* (how far the speaker strayed). Their own example of off-target speech: answering "Do you have any children?" with "Yes, two. They are both adopted." The second sentence counts as off-target, even though it is mild and indirectly relevant. — [Bluck et al. 2016](https://lifestorylab.psych.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/story-asides.pdf)
- **A modern version of the Arbuckle & Gold scale:** item verbosity scored 0/1 per answer and averaged; extent scored 0–9, anchored to the share of off-target content (about 5–10% = 1 point, about 90% = 9). Three coders, inter-rater reliability 0.89; item and extent measures agreed at 0.86. — [Yin & Peng 2016, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4844921/)
- **The core feature is lost focus, not talking a lot:** "The hallmark of OTV, in contrast to talkativeness, is the failure to maintain focus and coherence." — [Yin & Peng 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4844921/)
- **Younger adults also go off target, measurably:** at baseline, young adults scored lower than older adults on item verbosity (0.57 vs 0.75) and extent (2.55 vs 3.46 on the 0–9 scale). Young adults went *more* off target when distracting extra pictures were present (1.46 vs 1.22, p < .01); older adults did not change. — [Yin & Peng 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4844921/)
- **James, Burke, Austin & Hulme (1998), Psychology and Aging:** transcripts were scored by counting relevant and irrelevant words, with no further breakdown. — [Bluck et al. 2016](https://lifestorylab.psych.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/story-asides.pdf). From the abstract: older adults produced more off-topic speech for autobiographical topics but not for picture descriptions. A second sample of younger and older raters "rated older adults' story quality more positively than that of younger adults". **Rater age affected ratings of how focused the speech was**, "suggesting age differences in criteria" for off-topic speech. The authors read this as older adults choosing goals that emphasise "the significance of life experiences rather than conciseness" (Pragmatic Change hypothesis). — [James, Burke, Austin & Hulme 1998, "Production and perception of 'verbosity' in younger and older adults", Psychology and Aging 13(3) (abstract via Europe PMC, PMID 9793112)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9793112). Secondary sources further say the stories were rated more *interesting* and *informative*; that wording is not in the abstract and was not confirmed in the full text.
- **Indirectly relevant vs irrelevant:** Brandão & de Mattos Pimenta Parente (2009) coded propositions as relevant, indirectly relevant (feelings, causes, consequences) and irrelevant (unrelated opinions, the speaker's present state). Young adults gave more directly relevant propositions and older adults more indirectly relevant ones, with **no age difference in irrelevant propositions**. The effect appeared only in personal stories, not fictional ones. — [Bluck et al. 2016](https://lifestorylab.psych.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/story-asides.pdf)
- **Trunk & Abrams (2009), Psychology and Aging 24(2):324–337.** Speakers rated paired goals (for example *focus vs fascinating*), each pair having one "expressive" goal (suited to elaborative speech) and one "objective" goal (suited to concise speech). They then told episodic stories and procedural ("how to") accounts, rated by groups of younger and older listeners. Younger adults favoured expressive goals for episodic topics and objective goals for procedural topics; older adults showed no clear preference. Listener ratings: **episodic topics showed no difference (equally focused, coherent and clear); on procedural topics, older adults were "perceived as less focused, less clear, and more talkative".** Younger adults' goals predicted listeners' ratings of off-topic speech. — [Trunk & Abrams 2009 abstract, Europe PMC, PMID 19485651](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19485651)
- **Pushkar et al. (2000), "Social behavior and off-target verbosity in elderly people", Psychology and Aging 15(2).** 455 older adults screened, 198 in a get-acquainted conversation. Higher-OTV people "talked more, were less interested in their partners, and focused more on themselves", and **their conversational partners were less satisfied**. They talked less when shown boredom cues, but still more than others. Higher-OTV people were also worse at judging videotaped social interactions. OTV was unrelated to age and cognition in the low and mid range. — [Europe PMC, PMID 10879589](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10879589)
- **Arbuckle, Nohara-LeClair & Pushkar (2000), Psychology and Aging 15(1).** In a referential communication task (describing abstract figures to a partner, no personal topic), high-OTV directors (top 15% of 455 screened adults aged 63–93) were **"less efficient communicators"** and had poorer inhibitory control, but did not bring in personal information. So OTV costs the listener even outside storytelling. — [Europe PMC, PMID 10755290](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10755290)
- **Arbuckle & Gold (1993), Journal of Gerontology 48(5):P225.** 205 adults aged 61–90. OTV was defined as "extended speech that is lacking in focus or coherence". Tasks measuring the ability to suppress irrelevant information in working memory predicted verbosity; other cognitive measures did not. Psychosocial factors contributed independently. — [Europe PMC, PMID 8366267](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/8366267)
- **High OTV is a minority pattern.** Some low-level off-target talk "may be necessary to progress the conversation" (Gold & Arbuckle 1995, as cited). — [Bluck et al. 2016](https://lifestorylab.psych.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/story-asides.pdf); [Yin & Peng 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4844921/)
- **"Story asides" coding scheme** (world knowledge, biographical facts, life-story coherence, miscellaneous), N = 129 young and older adults. The scheme is reliable; older adults produce more asides only for autobiographical stories. The authors argue asides may be "a sign of communicative expertise". — [Bluck et al. 2016](https://lifestorylab.psych.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/story-asides.pdf)
- **Social cost of talking a lot (non-OTV, unpublished dissertation):** a talker's word count was stable across sessions. It correlated negatively with partners' ratings of the talker's listening skills and with partners' wish to work with them again. — [UC San Diego dissertation, "Talkativeness as a Component of Effective Communication & Impression Management" (search snippet only)](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/08s265kd)

### Inferences
- For a speaking app, the useful line is **irrelevant vs indirectly relevant vs core**, not "on vs off target". An example or a reason is indirectly relevant and can make an answer better. A tangent about something unrelated is the cost.
- The James et al. "better stories" finding comes from **storytelling** (life stories, rated as stories). Trunk & Abrams show the split directly: the same extra talk was not penalised in episodic stories but was rated less focused, less clear and more talkative in procedural ("how to") accounts. The app's Work questions (explain, recommend, justify) are closer to procedural or objective goals, where concision is what listeners reward. Everyday questions are closer to episodic ones, where some elaboration is welcome. Do not read James et al. as "rambling is fine".
- The best-supported listener dimensions to borrow for a judge or for tester ratings are Trunk & Abrams' **focused, coherent, clear, talkative**, plus Pushkar's **partner satisfaction**.
- The 0–9 "extent" scale (share of off-target content) is a simple model for a judge mark: "roughly what share of this answer didn't serve the question?"

### Gaps
- Only the abstracts of James et al. (1998), Trunk & Abrams (2009), Pushkar et al. (2000), Arbuckle et al. (2000) and Arbuckle & Gold (1993) were read (via Europe PMC). Effect sizes, rating scales and the exact OTV coding manuals were not retrieved.
- All OTV samples above are **older adults** (Trunk & Abrams and James et al. include younger speakers as a comparison group). The negative-partner and efficiency findings (Pushkar, Arbuckle 2000) concern the high-OTV extreme of older adults, which may reflect inhibition decline that fluent younger speakers don't share.
- No study found that rates **young, healthy, work-style answers** for OTV and links it to listener judgements of clarity or competence.

## 2. Correct Information Units (CIUs) and main concept analysis

### Takeaway
CIUs (Nicholas & Brookshire 1993) are the closest established measure to "words that did work for the listener". Each word is scored as conveying accurate, relevant information about the task or not. From that come % CIUs (informativeness per word) and CIUs per minute (efficiency). The method was built for aphasia, for monologue tasks with a known target (picture description, procedures). Its reliability falls apart in open conversation. Recent LLMs reach about 0.8 F1 with few-shot prompting but over-count CIUs. A version could work for a 60-second answer only if "relevant to *this* question" is defined per question.

### Cited Findings
- **The measures:** words per minute, CIUs per minute, and % CIUs (CIUs ÷ total words × 100). A rule-based system that measures informativeness and efficiency. CIUs per minute best separated adults with and without aphasia. — [Nicholas & Brookshire 1993, JSHR 36:338–350 (reference record)](https://utdanningsforskning.no/referanser2/2017/november/21/nicholas-l.-e.--brookshire-r.-h.-1993.-a-system-for-quantifying-the-informativeness-and-efficiency); [ASHA listing](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/jslhr.4203.636)
- **Normal speakers were part of the design:** the CIU system and the companion Main Concept analysis were developed comparing 20 non-brain-damaged adults with 20 adults with aphasia. The Main Concept analysis scores whether each expected key idea is present, accurate and complete. — [Nicholas & Brookshire 1995, main concepts](https://aphasia.talkbank.org/discourse/MainConcepts/Nicholas1995.pdf)
- **Reliability breaks down in conversation:** applied to the natural conversation of one person with aphasia, CIU intrarater reliability reached only 72% and interrater reliability was never above 63%. Disagreements came from scoring rules designed for single-speaker monologue. — [Oelschlaeger & Thorne 1999](https://talkbank.org/aphasia/access/English/NonProtocol/0docs/oelschlaeger1999b.pdf)
- **Machine learning:** supervised models separated word from non-word well, but CIU vs non-CIU was harder (best k-NN accuracy 0.824, AUC 0.787). — [Pittman et al., arXiv 2511.17553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.17553v1)
- **LLMs (2026):** on 16 "Cat Rescue" picture-description transcripts annotated with Nicholas & Brookshire rules, zero-shot prompting was insufficient. Few-shot gave mean F1 of about 0.78–0.82 for three models, but with **high recall and lower precision (systematic over-labelling of words as CIUs)**, and still too low for autonomous use. — [Do LLMs Reliably Identify Correct Information Units in Aphasic Discourse?, arXiv 2606.15696](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.15696) (abstract-level via search summary). A separate poster reported that locally run LLMs (best qwen2.5:7b) approximated human CIU scoring zero-shot, best in mild aphasia. — [NeuroLang 2026 poster](https://2026.neurolang.org/presentation/?id=1455). The two disagree on zero-shot performance.

### Inferences
- The CIU logic maps directly onto the owner's concern. **% CIUs is "share of words that did work"; CIUs per minute is "useful content per second".** Both allow for a long answer that is efficient, and both penalise a short answer that is mostly filler and repetition.
- The CIU rule of "relevant to the stimulus" works because picture tasks have a fixed target. An open question ("Tell me about a time…") does not. A practical version: plain code removes what can be counted (fillers, exact repeats, restarts). The judge then marks idea units, not single words, as core, support (reason, example, deliberate recap) or off-point. That is closer to Main Concept analysis and to the Brandão relevant/indirect/irrelevant split than to word-level CIUs.
- The LLM over-labelling bias (calling too much "useful") means an AI judge will likely *under*-report wordiness. Calibrate it against hand labels in `evals/`, as the project already does for the judge.
- Expect healthy speakers to sit near the ceiling on word-level % CIUs, since most of their words are accurate and relevant. Word-level CIUs would probably not separate a tight answer from a circling one. Idea-level repetition and off-point segments are what will vary. (Inference: healthy norms were not retrieved; see Gaps.)

### Gaps
- Published % CIU and CIUs-per-minute norms for non-brain-damaged adults (from Nicholas & Brookshire's tables) were not retrieved. The paper is paywalled.
- No study found that uses CIUs to coach or measure healthy fluent speakers, or for 60-second opinion or interview answers.
- Reliability figures for CIUs in the original structured tasks were not retrieved first-hand.

## 3. Grice's maxims and over-informative speech: do listeners penalise extra words?

### Takeaway
Mixed but fairly clear for short referring expressions. Speakers over-describe often (about 30% of trials). Listeners do **not** rate redundant detail as worse when asked directly, but their eyes show brief confusion. Later work found listeners do penalise over-information once design artifacts are removed. Extra words that **add a useful contrast** (for example colour in a colourful scene) speed listeners up. Over-information that serves the listener helps; over-information that doesn't is a small processing cost that people rarely notice consciously.

### Cited Findings
- **Production:** speakers over-described in about 30% of trials ("almost one-third of the time"). — [Engelhardt, Bailey & Ferreira 2006, JML 54:554–573](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Engelhardt-et-al.-2006_GriceanMaximQuantity_JML.pdf)
- **Ratings (1 = incorrect, 3 = adequate, 5 = perfect):** listeners strongly penalised *under*-description (bare instruction rated 3.12 when it was ambiguous vs 4.91 when it was sufficient). Over-described instructions were rated no worse than concise ones. In one-referent contexts, modified (longer) instructions were rated slightly *higher* on average than bare ones (4.52 vs 4.02). Abstract: listeners "do not judge over-descriptions to be any worse than concise expressions." — [Engelhardt et al. 2006](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Engelhardt-et-al.-2006_GriceanMaximQuantity_JML.pdf)
- **Online processing:** in a visual-world eye-tracking study, over-descriptions "trigger eye movements that can be interpreted as indicating confusion". The authors' conclusion: people are "only moderately Gricean". — [Engelhardt et al. 2006](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Engelhardt-et-al.-2006_GriceanMaximQuantity_JML.pdf)
- **Rebuttal:** Davies & Katsos (2013, Journal of Pragmatics 49:78–106) argued that Engelhardt's design favoured redundant modifiers. Once those features were removed, listeners **did** penalise over-informative expressions as well as under-informative ones, and speakers no longer over- or under-specified. — [White Rose eprint record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/78603/) (abstract only); reply: [Engelhardt et al., "Are listeners 'fully' Gricean?"](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/are-listeners-fully-gricean-a-comment-on-davies-and-katsos-2013/)
- **When redundancy helps:** redundant colour adjectives are often produced when colour contrast in the scene helps identify the object. Speakers do this more in colourful than in monochrome displays. Rubio-Fernández argues for an efficiency-based account (does the word make finding the referent easier?) over pure informativeness. — [Rubio-Fernández 2016, Frontiers in Psychology 7:153](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00153/full). Related: Arts, Maes, Noordman & Jansen (2011), "Overspecification facilitates object identification", Journal of Pragmatics 43(1):361–374 (cited there; not read). A Bayesian account of when "over-informative" expressions are rational: ["When redundancy is useful: A Bayesian approach to 'overinformative' referring expressions", arXiv 1903.08237](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.08237) (title only seen; authors and findings not confirmed this round). ERP evidence on "rational redundancy": [Tourtouri et al. 2021, Cognitive Science](https://publikationen.sulb.uni-saarland.de/bitstream/20.500.11880/32381/1/Cognitive%20Science%20-%202021%20-%20Tourtouri%20-%20Rational%20Redundancy%20in%20Referring%20Expressions%20%20Evidence%20from%20Event%e2%80%90related%20Potentials.pdf)
- Later reviews describe it as "still under debate whether over-specification hinders comprehension". — [Tourtouri et al. 2021](https://publikationen.sulb.uni-saarland.de/bitstream/20.500.11880/32381/1/Cognitive%20Science%20-%202021%20-%20Tourtouri%20-%20Rational%20Redundancy%20in%20Referring%20Expressions%20%20Evidence%20from%20Event%e2%80%90related%20Potentials.pdf)

### Inferences
- All of this is **single noun phrases in lab tasks** with healthy (mostly student) participants. That is good evidence for those participants, but the unit is tiny compared with a 60-second answer. The transferable principle is the test: **does the extra material make the listener's job easier?** Not: are there extra words?
- Under-informativeness (missing the point, the reason or the ending) is penalised much more reliably than over-informativeness. For the app, missing structure should stay the first priority, ahead of wordiness. This matches the current judge's clear-point marks.
- Gricean violations of *Relation* (off-point) and *Manner* (obscure, unordered, not brief) are named in the theory, but no empirical study measuring them in extended spoken answers was found.

### Gaps
- No empirical work found that measures Quantity, Relation or Manner violations at the scale of a whole spoken answer and links them to listener ratings.
- Davies & Katsos' exact rating numbers were not retrieved.

## 4. Conciseness or brevity and perceived competence or persuasiveness

### Takeaway
Weak evidence. No controlled study was found that varies spoken word count while holding content constant and measures perceived competence. The nearest evidence: listeners rate off-topic speakers on procedural topics as less focused, less clear and more talkative (Trunk & Abrams 2009); high-OTV speakers leave partners less satisfied (Pushkar et al. 2000); disfluency lowers perceived certainty; and an unpublished link between talkativeness and partners being less willing to work with someone again. These are ratings of clarity and liking, not of competence or persuasion.

### Cited Findings
- Filled pauses ("um", "uh") lowered perceived readiness and certainty regardless of expertise, though disfluent experts were judged more careful than disfluent novices. — [CogSci proceedings paper, eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8579j2fh/qt8579j2fh.pdf)
- Talkers' word counts correlated negatively with partners' ratings of their listening skills and with willingness to work with them again. — [UC San Diego dissertation (snippet)](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/08s265kd)
- Younger adults choose concise ("objective") goals for procedural topics, and listeners rated speakers who didn't as less focused, less clear and more talkative on those topics. — [Trunk & Abrams 2009 abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19485651)
- High-OTV older speakers left conversation partners less satisfied and were less efficient at conveying information about abstract figures. — [Pushkar et al. 2000](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10879589); [Arbuckle et al. 2000](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10755290)
- Counterpoint: older adults' longer, more off-target life stories were rated as better in story quality by young and old raters (James et al. 1998). — [James et al. 1998 abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9793112)

### Inferences
- The app's claim should be modest: "a tighter answer is easier to follow" (supported by the processing evidence in sections 1, 3 and 5), not "concise speakers are seen as more competent" (not established here).
- The context difference matters. Storytelling rewards rich asides; a work answer rewards getting to the point. This supports keeping the Work and Everyday categories and possibly using a softer wordiness bar for Everyday.

### Gaps
- No study found on spoken message length (holding content fixed) and perceived competence, credibility or persuasiveness. Persuasion research on "message length" (for example the elaboration likelihood model's length-as-cue findings) is about written arguments and was not searched.
- No research found on interview answers specifically (for example rated rambling in job interviews).
- A search summary claimed that moderate-to-faster speech rates are rated more competent, but the source paper could not be identified or verified, so it is left out.

## 5. Redundancy in spoken language: when restating or summarising helps recall

### Takeaway
Spoken organisational cues (previews, transitions, internal summaries, macro discourse markers) reliably help listeners record and recall lecture content, especially for higher-level structure. A summary that restates the content helps; one that only announces the topic does not. This is the strongest evidence that some "extra words" are not waste. They are signposts the listener needs because speech can't be re-read.

### Cited Findings
- Chaudron & Richards (1986), "The effect of discourse markers on the comprehension of lectures", Applied Linguistics 7(2):113–127: the foundational study. It found macro-markers (signals of topic shift and structure) helped L2 listeners more than micro-markers. — cited in [Tajabadi & Taghizadeh, IJPE](https://ijpe.inased.org/makale/2441) and [Martín del Pozo, Language Value 2019](https://doaj.org/article/37f272a0ec5f48ac974cbf9523240d46) (original not read)
- Flowerdew & Tauroza found listeners who heard a lecture **with** micro-markers did significantly better than those without, which conflicts in part with Chaudron & Richards. — [IJPE](https://ijpe.inased.org/makale/2441)
- 80 Korean EFL learners: the group hearing a lecture with signals "recalled significantly more high- as well as low-level information … than the unsignaled group." — [Atlantis Press paper](https://download.atlantis-press.com/article/25884346.pdf)
- Titsworth & Kiewra (2004), "Spoken organizational lecture cues and student notetaking", Contemporary Educational Psychology 29:447–461. In the underlying dissertation, organisational cues increased the details and structural points students noted, and notes taken on cued lectures gave the best long-term achievement. — [Titsworth 1999 dissertation](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/dissertations/AAI9942159) (the 2004 article was not read; a widely repeated "45% more details" figure could not be traced to a source and should not be used)
- Summaries: an *informative* summary (restating the content) improved audience retention; an *indicative* summary (saying only what was covered) did not. — [Leiden University thesis](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3185883/view) (student thesis, weak evidence)
- Public-speaking textbooks recommend "planned redundancy" (previews, internal summaries, conclusions) to help listeners retain content. — [Pittsburgh Comm transitions guide](https://comm.pitt.edu/transitions); [eCampus Ontario](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/persuasion/chapter/transitions/) (advice, not evidence)

### Inferences
- Most of this evidence is from **lectures** (10+ minutes) and often from second-language listeners, who benefit most from structure. In a 60-second answer the listener's memory load is much smaller, so a full preview plus a recap would usually be overhead. What probably transfers: **one signpost and one closing restatement of the point that adds a conclusion** (an "informative" summary). Repeating the same sentence verbatim mid-answer probably does not count.
- Design rule this supports: a deliberate recap at the end that restates the main point is useful (it is also the "proper ending" the judge already marks). The same point restated three times in the middle, with nothing new, is circling.

### Gaps
- No study found on restatement or summary effects in short (1–2 minute) spoken answers or with native-speaker listeners in conversation.
- The Chaudron & Richards and Titsworth & Kiewra numbers were not retrieved first-hand.

## 6. Automated and LLM-based measures of verbosity, relevance or topic drift

### Takeaway
There is one well-validated automated measure of topic drift in 60-second spoken answers: Hoffman et al.'s LSA "global coherence". It uses the same response length as the app, works for young adults, and is validated against human ratings. LLM-based CIU scoring is emerging but over-generous. No validated automated "verbosity" or "redundancy" measure for healthy speakers was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Hoffman, Loginova & Russell (2018), eLife:** 30 young (18–30) and 30 older (61–91) adults, 14 prompts, **60-second spoken answers each** (for example "Which is your favourite season and why?"), 840 samples. — [PMC6150697](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6150697/)
  - **Method:** latent semantic analysis vectors (British National Corpus, 300 dimensions). Each answer is split into 20-word windows moving one word at a time. *Global coherence* = cosine similarity of each window to a composite of other people's answers to the same prompt. *Local coherence* = similarity to the previous window. Scores are averaged and multiplied by 100.
  - **Validity:** global coherence correlated with human ratings at r = 0.68 (0.71 with same-age composites); local coherence only r = 0.37. Reliability across prompts α = 0.83; test-retest r = 0.88 (global).
  - **Length effect:** answers with more words (in a fixed 60 s) had significantly **lower** global coherence (B ≈ −0.9, p ≈ .014–.019). People who said more tended to drift further from the topic. Length did not predict local coherence.
  - Older adults were less globally coherent; semantic selection ability explained the age gap.
- LLM CIU scoring: few-shot F1 about 0.78–0.82 with over-labelling of CIUs; mixed zero-shot results. — [arXiv 2606.15696](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.15696); [NeuroLang 2026 poster](https://2026.neurolang.org/presentation/?id=1455)
- Supervised ML CIU detection: accuracy 0.824 at best. — [arXiv 2511.17553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.17553v1)

### Inferences
- Hoffman's design is a near-exact fit for the app (60-second spoken answers to everyday prompts, including young adults). A modern version could compare windows of the transcript against **the question plus a reference answer** using sentence embeddings, giving a "how much of this answer stayed on the question" curve over time. This would be plain code with an embedding model. Its main limitation: an example or anecdote can be semantically distant from the question and still be useful, so it would mark useful examples as drift. That is exactly the false positive the owner wants to avoid. Use it as a signal for the judge, not as a shown score.
- Two cheap, countable proxies that don't need AI and don't punish examples: (a) **time to first statement of the main point** (already in the project's voice examples, "your point came at 40 seconds"); (b) **near-duplicate restatements** (high similarity between non-adjacent sentences, excluding the final sentence so a deliberate closing recap isn't penalised). Both are inferences, not validated measures.
- An LLM judge asked to label idea units as core / support / off-point / repeat, with quoted evidence, is the most direct way to measure "words that did no work". Given the over-labelling bias found in CIU studies, it needs hand labels in `evals/` and should be tested for run-to-run consistency, as the current judge is.

### Gaps
- No validated automated measure of verbosity or redundancy for healthy fluent speakers was found. No ACL Anthology work on "rambling" in spoken answers was found in this round.
- Not checked: whether Hoffman's measure has been redone with sentence embeddings or LLMs, or used on interview or work answers.
- No study found that compares automated drift scores with listener judgements of *usefulness* (as opposed to coherence).
