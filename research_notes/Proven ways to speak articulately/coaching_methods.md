# Coaching methods for articulate impromptu speech

Scope note: about 20 searches and fetches. Many coach claims come from secondary summaries (magazine articles, book summary sites), not the books themselves. Where only a summary was seen, that is said. Two official pages (Stanford GSB insight article, toastmasters.org Table Topics page) returned 404 and could not be read directly.

Evidence labels used below:
- **[Tested]**: at least one controlled study or meta-analysis found.
- **[Weak evidence]**: pre/post or field studies only, no randomized control.
- **[Popular advice]**: no testing found in this research.

## 1. Matt Abrahams (Stanford GSB): What? So What? Now What? and his other drills

### Takeaway
Abrahams teaches a short sequence: calm the body, lower the bar ("dare to be dull"), listen for what is actually being asked, then answer with a fixed structure. His main structure is What? So What? Now What? (your point, why it matters to the listener, the next step). **[Popular advice]**: no study of the structure itself was found.

### Cited Findings
- His steps, as printed in Toastmasters Magazine (Sept 2023):
  - **Step 1, manage anxiety:** slow belly breaths with the out-breath longer than the in-breath, big gestures, step toward the audience, hold something cold in your hands.
  - **Step 2, lower the pressure:** there is no single right answer; the improv rule "Dare to be dull"; focus on serving the listener; a mantra such as "I have value to bring."
  - **Step 3, see what is needed:** treat it as a conversation, not a test; listen to tone, pace and body language; pause, breathe and get curious before answering.
  - **Step 4, What? So What? Now What?:** say your point, say why it matters to the listener, give next steps. **Drill:** map emails, articles and documents you read onto this structure.
  - **Step 5, Know, Feel, Do:** decide what the audience should know, feel and do, and use that to choose content.
  - **Plain language:** no acronyms or jargon; beware the "curse of knowledge."
  - Source: [Toastmasters Magazine, Sept 2023](https://toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2023/sept/impromptu-speaking)
- He calls What-So What-Now What his favorite structure, and uses it for answers, feedback and even small talk. His reason: the brain follows a logical order much better than a list or rambling. — [Toastmasters Magazine](https://toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2023/sept/impromptu-speaking); [Roland Berger Think:Act interview](https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/Matt-Abrahams-on-speaking-well-when-on-the-spot.html)
- Secondary write-ups list PREP (Point, Reason, Example, Point) and Problem-Solution-Benefit as other structures he endorses, and call What-So What-Now What the "Swiss Army knife" of structures. He warns that bullet-point thinking misses the point in spontaneous speech. — [Entrepreneur](https://entm.ag/SwHbek); [National CIO Review](https://nationalcioreview.com/articles-insights/master-the-moment-how-to-communicate-without-a-script/)
- His "dare to be dull and imperfect" message is also covered in [The Globe and Mail](https://arc-dev.theglobeandmail.com/business/careers/management/article-dare-to-be-dull-and-imperfect-in-your-impromptu-public-speaking-at) (that link is a mirror copy, not the main site).

### Inferences
- What-So What-Now What, PREP, Problem-Solution-Benefit and BLUF all share one rule: **say the point first, then support it, then close.** This is the strongest point of agreement in this research.
- Know-Feel-Do is a planning tool for content more than a way to order spoken sentences.

### Gaps
- Problem-Solution-Benefit was not seen in Abrahams' own words. Only secondary summaries name it.
- His books ("Think Faster, Talk Smarter", 2023; "Speaking Up Without Freaking Out") and the podcast transcripts were not read directly. The Stanford GSB article URL tried returned 404.
- No study was found that tests What-So What-Now What, or any named structure, on impromptu answers.

## 2. Toastmasters Table Topics

### Takeaway
Table Topics is the standard impromptu drill: you get a topic you have not seen and speak for **1 to 2 minutes**. Timing lights show green at 1:00, yellow at 1:30 and red at 2:00. Studies of Toastmasters are small pre/post or survey studies. **[Weak evidence]** for less anxiety, and no evidence specific to articulacy.

### Cited Findings
- Table Topics run 1 to 2 minutes. Evaluations run 2 to 3 minutes. Timing cards: green at 1 minute, yellow at 1:30, red at 2:00. The clock starts on the speaker's first word. Some clubs add a 30-second grace period. These are club-level scripts, not official Toastmasters documents. — [MIT Toastmasters timer role](https://web.mit.edu/toastmasters/roles/timer.html); [EdUHK timekeeper briefing](https://www.eduhk.hk/cle2/editor_images/documents/clubied/Toastmaster/Briefing%20for%20Time%20keeper.pdf)
- Abrahams names Table Topics as a place to practice Know-Feel-Do. — [Toastmasters Magazine](https://toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2023/sept/impromptu-speaking)
- **Evidence:** a 2022 review by Kalińska says "there is little academic research" on the Toastmasters method. The studies it found cover three themes: Toastmasters as an education program, social ease among members, and public speaking anxiety. — [Kalińska review (CEEOL)](https://ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1116252)
- A modified Toastmasters workshop at the University of Szczecin (weekly, 2 hours, 2014/15) reported less language anxiety and better speaking skills, using mixed methods. — [Kalińska, UMCS](https://journals.umcs.pl/ff/article/view/13239)
- University Toastmasters clubs in Pakistan were studied as a way to reduce speech anxiety in English learners (Irfan and Zafar, 2020). — [Linguistics and Literature Review](https://journals.umt.edu.pk/index.php/llr/article/view/955)
- A Taiwanese thesis surveyed 89 members and interviewed 12. It found clubs a supportive place with many chances to speak. — [NTUST thesis](https://etheses.lib.ntust.edu.tw/thesis/detail/81fc4a0ed7445a8503c5d67b2307096a/?seq=5)

### Inferences
- 1 to 2 minutes is the most common length used for impromptu practice. A 60-second cap sits at the short end of that range.
- The Toastmasters evidence is about anxiety and confidence, mostly in second-language speakers. It does not measure clarity or filler words.

### Gaps
- The official toastmasters.org Table Topics page returned 404. Official evaluation criteria for Table Topics (for example contest judging guides) were not found.
- No randomized controlled study of Toastmasters was found.

## 3. Structures: PREP, STAR, Pyramid Principle, BLUF, rule of three, past-present-future, answer first

### Takeaway
Nearly every structure puts the main point first. Minto's Pyramid Principle (McKinsey) and the US Army's BLUF are the formal versions of the idea. **[Popular advice]** for spoken use. Writing is where these rules are written down, and no study of them in speech was found. The one tested piece nearby is the working-memory argument behind the rule of three, which Gallo cites.

### Cited Findings
- **Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto, McKinsey):** a top-down method. The governing thought (the answer) comes first, grouped arguments sit below it, and data sits at the bottom. It opens with **SCQA**: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. Arguments are grouped so they don't overlap and nothing is missing (MECE). For clarity under pressure, a list of similar reasons is preferred over a chain of logic. — [ThinkInsights](https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/pyramid-principle); [RoadToOffer](https://www.roadtooffer.com/blog/pyramid-principle); [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/the-pyramid-principle-case-interview/)
  - Sources disagree on the date: some say it was developed at McKinsey in the 1960s, others give 1985 for the book. — same sources
  - In case interviews it is used for recommendations, not for personal "fit" stories. — [RoadToOffer](https://www.roadtooffer.com/blog/pyramid-principle)
- **BLUF (US Army):** Army Regulation 25-50 requires the main point at the start and the active voice. Memos open with a short purpose sentence, then the recommendation or key fact. Sources link the rule to the Plain Writing Act of 2010. One source says the BLUF requirement first came from a DA pamphlet that has since been rescinded. — [Wikipedia: BLUF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication)); [JMU "Writing in the Army Style"](https://www.jmu.edu/uwc/tutor-resources/MSL202L15%20Writing%20in%20the%20Army%20Style.pdf); [LegalClarity](https://legalclarity.org/bottom-line-up-front-bluf-what-it-is-and-how-to-use-it/)
  - The claim that BLUF became the Army standard in 1988 comes from a vendor blog and was not checked. — [AirOps](https://www.airops.com/blog/bottom-line-up-front-bluf)
- **Rule of three (Carmine Gallo):** it appears throughout the best-known TED talks. Gallo's reason is that short-term (working) memory holds about three chunks, and he suggests outlining a talk as three stories. — [Shortform summary of Gallo](https://www.shortform.com/blog/effective-speech/); [Carmine Gallo: Talk Like TED takeaways](https://www.carminegallo.com/cracking-the-ted-code-takeaways-from-talk-like-ted-the-9-public-speaking-secrets-of-the-worlds-top-minds/)
- **PREP (Point, Reason, Example, Point)** appears in coverage of Abrahams' advice. — [National CIO Review](https://nationalcioreview.com/articles-insights/master-the-moment-how-to-communicate-without-a-script/)

### Inferences
- **Answer-first is where the most sources agree:** Abrahams (What first), PREP (Point first), Minto (governing thought first), Army (BLUF), TED (one-sentence throughline, see section 5). The app's "clear point" mark (main point, reason or example, proper ending) is close to PREP.
- Rule of three and PREP both limit support to a few items. That keeps a 60-second answer from turning into a list.

### Gaps
- **STAR** (Situation, Task, Action, Result, the interview structure) and **past-present-future** were not sourced in this session. Both are common in coaching material, but no citation was collected.
- No study was found that compares answer-first with other orders for how well listeners understand spoken impromptu answers.
- AR 25-50 itself was not read. Its claims come from secondary sources.

## 4. Improv-based training (Second City, Stanford, "yes, and")

### Takeaway
Improv is taught at Stanford GSB by Dan Klein and is behind Abrahams' "dare to be dull." Its best evidence is for **tolerating uncertainty and well-being**, not for speaking clearly. **[Tested]** (randomized lab sessions) for uncertainty tolerance and divergent thinking. **[Weak evidence]** (field study) for anxiety. No randomized trial on public-speaking anxiety was found.

### Cited Findings
- Dan Klein teaches improv full time at Stanford, in Drama, the GSB and the d.school. At the GSB he co-teaches "Acting With Power" with Deb Gruenfeld, on status behavior. He also teaches an executive course on the "improviser's mindset." — [Stanford GSB: Dan Klein](https://gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/faculty/dan-m-klein); [GSB "Back to Class: Creativity Workout"](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/experience/news-history/back-class-creativity-workout)
- University of Michigan work (Felsman): in randomized lab experiments, a short improv session improved uncertainty tolerance, divergent thinking and mood, compared with a social-interaction control. A field study of 350 Detroit students in grades 8 to 12 (10 weekly sessions) found social anxiety went down, with no random control group. — [Deep Blue dissertation](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/handle/2027.42/151483); [Michigan News](https://detroit.umich.edu/news-stories/improv-course-may-help-teens-learn-to-tolerate-uncertainty/)
- Northern Michigan University reports improv training reduced social anxiety. — [NMU News](https://news.nmu.edu/improv-training-can-reduce-social-anxiety)
- A University of New Brunswick project is testing an improv course for people with mild to moderate public-speaking anxiety. The researcher notes there is limited research so far. — [LiveWire Calgary, Aug 2026](https://livewirecalgary.com/2026/08/28/calgary-scholar-studies-improv-as-a-solution-to-public-speaking-anxiety/)
- University of South Australia studied an adapted theatre-sports approach for stuttering. — [UniSA, March 2024](https://unisa.edu.au/media-centre/Releases/2024/a-twist-on-theatre-sports-could-counteract-a-stutter)

### Inferences
- In improv, "yes, and" and "dare to be dull" are about lowering the bar to start talking. That is a different job from structuring the answer, so the two fit together (start without fear, then use a structure).

### Gaps
- No course called "Improvisationally Speaking" was found at Stanford. The name may be wrong, or it may be a workshop or podcast title.
- Second City's corporate curriculum was not researched.
- No study was found that measures filler words or clarity after improv training.

## 5. Other well-known coaches

### Takeaway
Most famous coaches add delivery advice (pause, vary pace, cut fillers) and listener-centered intent on top of answer-first. Almost none of their specific methods have been tested. **[Popular advice]** throughout.

### Cited Findings
- **Julian Treasure (TED, "How to speak so that people want to listen"):** seven "deadly sins" to avoid: gossip, judging, negativity, complaining, excuses or blaming, exaggeration or lying, and dogmatism (sources list them slightly differently). The antidote is **HAIL**: Honesty (be straight and clear), Authenticity (be yourself), Integrity (be your word), Love (wish people well). — [Cornerstone summary](https://www.cornerstoneondemand.com/resources/article/ted-talk-tuesday-how-speak-so-others-listen/); [Suzi McAlpine](https://suzimcalpine.com/the-7-deadly-sins-of-speaking/)
- **Vinh Giang:** his vocal basics are rate, volume, pitch and melody, tonality (emotion in the voice) and the pause. He calls silence the "most important note," says to replace "um" with a pause, and warns that loud is not the same as engaging. **Drill:** record a 5-minute video on any topic, then review it three ways: listen, watch your movement, and transcribe it to count fillers. — [ISACA article](https://www.isaca.org/resources/news-and-trends/newsletters/atisaca/2021/volume-37/the-magic-trick-to-gaining-influence); [Zain's World summary](https://zains-world.beehiiv.com/p/stop-being-invisible-the-secret-code-to-communication-confidence-in-3-6-months)
- **Jefferson Fisher ("The Next Conversation"):** cut filler words and apologetic phrases, use breathing and short pauses to stay in control, say fewer words, and treat confidence as the result of being assertive, not a requirement for it. Only summaries and publisher text were seen. — [Penguin Random House workbook](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/790497/the-next-conversation-workbook-by-jefferson-fisher/audio/); [Aspire podcast insights](https://podcast-insider.on-forge.com/blog/aspire-with-emma-grede/aspire-insights-how-to-speak-so-people-will-listen-with-communication-expert-jefferson-fisher); [Diary of a CEO episode](https://www.blackcountryradio.co.uk/podcasts/the-diary-of-a-ceo-with-steven-bartlett/episode/the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-stop-saying-this-word-its-making-you-sound-weak-the-more-you-do-this-the-more-you-sound-like-a-liar)
- **Chris Anderson (TED Talks: The Official Guide):** every talk needs a **throughline**, the one idea the audience should leave with, which fits in one sentence. Avoid obvious throughlines. — [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/ted-talks-the-official-guide-to-public-speaking/)
- **Carmine Gallo:** rule of three; one "shocking" or memorable moment (a statistic, image or story) that drives home the throughline. — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/effective-speech/); [Carmine Gallo](https://www.carminegallo.com/talking-ted-style-5-steps-to-a-winning-presentation-business-news-daily/)
- **Randy Olson, ABT (And, But, Therefore)** from "Houston, We Have a Narrative" (2018): replace "and, and, and" fact-listing with context (And), a conflict or problem (But), and a resolution (Therefore). Caltech's writing center adapts it as Context, Problem, Solution. — [SESYNC toolkit](https://www.sesync.org/resources/communications-toolkit-and-therefore-statement); [Caltech handout](https://writing.caltech.edu/documents/30450/Scientific_Storytelling_Handout.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Pause instead of filler** comes up independently from Giang, Fisher and Abrahams (pause and breathe before answering). That makes it the second strongest point of agreement after answer-first, and it has partial research support (section 7).
- **Say less** (Fisher; Abrahams' plain language; Anderson's one-sentence throughline) is a third point of agreement.

### Gaps (not sourced in this session, so no claims made)
- Charisma on Command, Nancy Duarte (Resonate, "what is / what could be"), Patricia Fripp, Dale Carnegie, Winston Churchill's preparation habits, and British Parliamentary debate (speech lengths, points of information) were not researched. Their methods are well known in coaching circles, but nothing was collected here to cite.
- The quote attributed to Mark Twain, "It usually takes me more than three weeks to prepare a good impromptu speech", was searched but not confirmed. Treat the attribution as unverified.
- Randy Olson's claim of a "narrative index" (counting But and Therefore words) was not found in a source.

## 6. Specific drills: one sentence first, word of the day, pause instead of filler, record and review, random topics, ABT, headline first

### Takeaway
**Record and review** is the only drill with real controlled evidence (a meta-analysis of video feedback). **Awareness training to cut fillers** has a behavior-analysis research line behind it. The rest are popular advice.

### Cited Findings
- **Record and review [Tested]:**
  - A meta-analysis of 12 published experiments found that video feedback in public speaking courses gave better speech content, more skill gained, better test scores and better attitudes, with no significant rise in anxiety. — [OJP abstract: effect of videotape feedback](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/effect-videotape-feedback-development-presentation-skills); [U. Dayton BCCA](https://ecommons.udayton.edu/bcca/vol25/iss1/13)
  - Setting a goal before watching, plus your own feedback from the video, beat simply rewatching. Video self-review also made students more accurate judges of their own speeches and less likely to overrate them (two studies, N = 102 and N = 622). — [Missouri State BearWorks](https://bearworks.missouristate.edu/articles-coal/349/); [U. Dayton BCCA vol 28](https://ecommons.udayton.edu/bcca/vol28/iss1/13)
  - On anxiety the results are mixed. A 1994 study found students shown their own videos did not get less anxious, while students not shown them did. A Chinese EFL study found video feedback lowered anxiety and improved delivery more than spoken feedback alone. — [U. Dayton BCCA vol 6](https://ecommons.udayton.edu/bcca/vol6/iss1/18); [Liu and Aryadoust (ALTAANZ)](https://www.altaanz.org/uploads/5/9/0/8/5908292/2._sila_11_2__liu_aryadoust.pdf)
- **Awareness training for nervous habits and fillers [Tested, small-N]:** a behavior-analysis research line uses awareness training to reduce nervous habits and filler words during public speaking. Only the title was seen. — [Behaviorist Book Club: awareness training](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/aba-fundamentals/using-awareness-training-to-decrease-nervous-habits-during-public-spea/)
- **Map what you read onto What-So What-Now What** (Abrahams). — [Toastmasters Magazine](https://toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2023/sept/impromptu-speaking)
- **One-sentence throughline or headline first** (Anderson; BLUF). — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/ted-talks-the-official-guide-to-public-speaking/); [Wikipedia: BLUF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication))
- **ABT one-liner:** turn your point into "And ..., but ..., therefore ...". — [SESYNC](https://www.sesync.org/resources/communications-toolkit-and-therefore-statement)
- **Random-topic practice:** Table Topics itself (section 2).

### Inferences
- The app's core loop (record, get specific feedback, try again) matches the best-supported drill: video and self-review combined with goal setting. A clear goal for the retry (one tip) matches the finding that goal-directed review beats plain replay.

### Gaps
- No source was collected for a "word of the day" vocabulary drill, or for any test of it.
- No study of "pause instead of filler" as a drill was found. Only the listener-perception studies in section 7 relate to it.

## 7. Vocabulary precision, hedging, conciseness, and fillers: what coaches say and what research shows

### Takeaway
Coaches say cut fillers, cut hedges and apologies, and use plain words. Research partly agrees. **Hedges usually lower credibility and persuasion**, with the size of the effect depending on how expert the speaker is. **Fillers matter at high rates:** about 12 a minute hurt ratings, 5 or fewer did not in one 2024 study. Some studies find occasional "uh" or "um" harmless or even helpful, so "zero fillers" is not supported as a goal.

### Cited Findings
- **Hedges [Tested]:** messages with hedges led to less persuasion, worse views of the source and weaker ratings of the argument than versions without hedges (reviewed in Durik et al., 2008). — [Durik et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%206/DurikETAL2008.pdf)
  - In a mock courtroom, few hedges and hesitations gave the best ratings for authority and likability (Hosman and Wright, 1987). — [USM Aquila](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16292)
  - Speaker expertise changes how hedges affect perceived authority (Smith, Siltanen and Hosman, 1998). Effects on persuasion are mixed across studies (Hosman, Huebner and Siltanen, 2002). — [USM Aquila 16286](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16286); [Hosman, Huebner and Siltanen 2002 PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%206/HosmanHuebnerSiltanen2002.pdf)
  - Tag questions combined with argument quality shaped perceived power and credibility (Hosman and Siltanen, 2011). The source's credibility changes whether tag questions come across as weak at all (Blankenship and Craig, 2007). — [USM Aquila 437](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/437); [Blankenship and Craig 2007 PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%206/BlankenshipCraig2007.pdf)
- **Filler words [Tested]:**
  - Laske and DiGennaro Reed (2024), Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, "Um, so, like, do speech disfluencies matter?" (DOI 10.1002/jaba.1093): speeches with 0, 5 or 12 fillers a minute. 12 a minute lowered ratings of effectiveness; 5 or fewer did not. Only secondary summaries were seen. — [Behaviorist Book Club summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/aba-fundamentals/um--so--like--do-speech-disfluencies-matter--a-parametric-evaluation-o/); [ABA this week listing](https://fixedinterval.substack.com/p/aba-this-week-651)
  - In spontaneous speech, filled pauses lowered how ready and certain the speaker seemed, whatever their expertise. Listeners were less likely to choose the disfluent speaker again, and that ran through perceived readiness. — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8579j2fh)
  - An older study: "um"-filled pauses were rated better than silent pauses, but no pauses at all rated best. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02175503)
  - Survey interviewers who paused now and then (including with "uh" or "um") got more people to agree than those who never paused. Seen only in secondary coverage. — [Next Big Idea Club (Susan Cain)](https://nextbigideaclub.com/magazine/susan-cain-dont-need-drop-um-great-speaker/7105/amp/)
- **Coaches on conciseness and plain words:** Abrahams (no jargon, curse of knowledge), Fisher (fewer words, no apologetic phrases), Anderson (one-sentence throughline), Army BLUF (plain writing, active voice). — sources in sections 1, 3, 5

### Inferences
- A coaching target such as "fewer than about 5 fillers a minute" is easier to defend than "zero fillers." That has a single-study basis and needs checking against the original paper.
- Hedging advice should depend on context: hedges cost the most when the listener doesn't yet see the speaker as an expert.

### Gaps
- No research was found on "vocabulary precision" as such, meaning whether more exact word choice improves listener ratings of impromptu speech.
- The full Laske 2024 paper (sample size, effect sizes) was not read.

## 8. How long should an impromptu answer be?

### Takeaway
The best-known practice format, Table Topics, uses **1 to 2 minutes**, with green at 1:00 marking the minimum. No research found sets a best length for an impromptu answer.

### Cited Findings
- Table Topics: 1 to 2 minutes, green at 1:00, yellow at 1:30, red at 2:00, and some clubs allow 30 seconds of grace. — [MIT Toastmasters](https://web.mit.edu/toastmasters/roles/timer.html); [EdUHK](https://www.eduhk.hk/cle2/editor_images/documents/clubied/Toastmaster/Briefing%20for%20Time%20keeper.pdf)
- Coaches favor brevity in general ("say fewer words", Fisher; one-sentence throughline, Anderson; key point first, BLUF). — sources in sections 3 and 5

### Inferences
- A 60-second cap (this app) is at Toastmasters' minimum. It suits a three-part structure (point, one reason or example, close) better than full PREP plus several examples. That fits the rule-of-three and working-memory reasoning.

### Gaps
- Time limits for British Parliamentary debate and for interview answer norms (for example STAR answers of about 2 minutes) were not sourced in this session.
- No study was found linking answer length to listener ratings of clarity.
