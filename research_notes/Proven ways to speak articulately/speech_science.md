# Speech science: what measurably improves fluency, clarity and organization in impromptu speaking

Scope note: almost all of the controlled intervention research below is on second-language (L2) learners. Native-speaker evidence is mostly about *perception* (what listeners judge as fluent), not about training. Each section flags L2-only findings and how far they plausibly carry over to fluent native speakers. Effect sizes are given only where a source I retrieved states them; numbers I could not verify go under Gaps.

## Task repetition: does repeating the same speaking task help, and does it carry over to new topics?

### Takeaway
Repeating the same task reliably improves fluency on that task, with most of the gain in the first 2–4 repetitions. A 2025 meta-analysis finds the effect is real but smaller for fluency than for grammatical complexity and accuracy, and smaller when repetitions are spaced further apart. Carry-over to new topics is supported by one small but well-designed experiment (de Jong & Perfetti 2011) and is not yet established in meta-analyses.

### Cited Findings
- **Meta-analysis (strong, but L2):** Abdi Tabari, Zhuang & Farahanynia (2025), "Task repetition and L2 oral performance: A meta-analysis", *System* 135, 103868. Task repetition has a "substantial" effect on syntactic complexity and accuracy. Fluency and lexical complexity also improve, but by smaller amounts. Effects were larger in foreign-language than in second-language settings, exact repetition and narrative tasks worked best for complexity, and **longer spacing between repetitions went with smaller effects on fluency and complexity**. I saw only the abstract, so the number of studies and pooled effect sizes are not given here — [Hildesheim repository record](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19120)
- **Companion meta-analysis on writing (for comparison):** 31 studies; fluency d = 0.50, accuracy d = 1.19; more repetitions gave larger effects (2025, *Journal of Second Language Writing*; per search summary, likely the Hildesheim record below, not checked) — [Hildesheim repository](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19111)
- **Lambert, Kormos & Minn (2017), *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 39(1), 167–196.** 32 Japanese learners of English, at three proficiency levels, each did three monologue tasks (instruction, narration, opinion) **six times**. Speech rate rose most across the first three performances and kept rising until the fifth. Filled pauses and self-repairs fell; self-repairs fell only after the fourth performance. **Clause-final pauses fell until the 2nd performance; mid-clause pauses kept falling until the 4th.** Gains held "regardless of proficiency level or task type." The authors read the different timelines as repetition helping different stages of speech production (conceptualizing first, then formulating, then monitoring) — [Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/task-repetition-and-second-language-speech-processing/0EA95A4C7D9E90CD2AB30043F84A4635)
- **Transfer to new topics: de Jong & Perfetti (2011), *Language Learning* 61(2), 533–568.** 24 ESL students did three training sessions of the 4/3/2 technique. One group repeated the same topic across the 4-, 3- and 2-minute speeches; the other changed topic each round. **Both groups got more fluent during training, but only the repeating group kept the gains on a post-test with a new topic.** The repeating group also reused more words across speeches, mostly words not specific to the topic. The authors interpret this as proceduralization (knowledge becoming automatic, in ACT-R terms) — [LearnLab project summary](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fostering_fluency_in_second_language_learning); [full PDF](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/perfettilab/pubpdfs/de%20Jong%20Language%20Learning.pdf)
- **Mixed results exist:** a Korean EFL study found no significant rise in speech rate after task repetition in either group (KAIST record, "What needs to be repeated during task-based interaction?") — [KAIST](https://koasas.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/244721)
- Repetition only "marginally" improved fluency in a study crossing repetition with task enjoyment (*Language Teaching*) — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/interaction-between-task-repetition-and-oral-task-enjoyment-effects-on-speech-complexity-accuracy-and-fluency/D8C1FD395F5275FF3F3AC2356FB15A45)

### Inferences
- The most robust single-session finding is that a 2nd and 3rd attempt at the same question is clearly more fluent than the 1st, with diminishing returns after about the 4th. This supports a "try again" flow; a 2nd attempt captures much of the immediate gain.
- Same-day gains on the same question are partly rehearsal and should not be presented as general improvement. De Jong & Perfetti suggest repetition can transfer, but the evidence is one study of 24 people.
- The mechanism (freeing attention from finding words so it can go to structure) is about how speech is produced, not about the language being foreign. It plausibly applies to native speakers answering an unfamiliar question, though the gain is likely smaller because native word retrieval is already automatic. No native-speaker repetition trial was found.
- Earlier attempts reduced clause-boundary pauses (planning what to say) before mid-clause pauses (finding words), which hints that the first retry mainly helps organization.

### Gaps
- Bygate's foundational task-repetition studies (Bygate 1996, 2001) were not retrieved; their findings are not reported here.
- Ahmadian's studies (e.g., Ahmadian & Tavakoli 2011, *Language Teaching Research*) were not retrieved.
- The pooled fluency effect size and the number of studies in the 2025 oral meta-analysis were not available in the abstract.
- No study found on task repetition with native speakers.

## The 4/3/2 technique and time pressure

### Takeaway
Giving the same talk three times in less and less time (4, then 3, then 2 minutes; Nation 1989) improves fluency more than repeating it with constant time. It does not by itself improve complexity or accuracy. Evidence comes from small L2 classroom studies (n = 20–24), not meta-analyses.

### Cited Findings
- **Thai & Boers (2016), *TESOL Quarterly* 50, 369–393.** 20 Vietnamese EFL students gave the same talk three times, with shrinking time (4/3/2) or constant time (3/3/3). "Fluency was enhanced most markedly in the shrinking-time condition", with no significant change in complexity or accuracy in that condition. Accuracy gains have been seen under constant time but not shrinking time (this point is per search summary of secondary sources) — [MetaTOC listing](https://metatoc.com/papers/74068-repeating-a-monologue-under-increasing-time-pressure-effects-on-fluency-complexity-and-accuracy); [summary in later review](https://journals.umz.ac.ir/article_6115.html)
- Earlier claims that 4/3/2 also improves syntactic complexity were not corroborated by later studies (Boers 2014; Thai & Boers 2016), according to a later summary (per search summary; source page not checked)
- **Tran & Saito (2021), *Language Teaching Research*.** Adding delayed, explicit correction after 4/3/2 ("4/3/2+AE") improved overall fluency **and** accuracy, across different topics — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10128571/1/LTR2021.pdf)
- De Jong & Perfetti (2011) used 4/3/2; see the section above for transfer (24 participants) — [PDF](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/perfettilab/pubpdfs/de%20Jong%20Language%20Learning.pdf)

### Inferences
- Time pressure pushes speakers to drop hesitations and say things more compactly. It trades against accuracy and elaboration. For "getting to the point" that may be the desired effect, but nobody has tested it with native speakers.
- Tran & Saito suggest the best combination is repetition under time pressure followed by short, explicit feedback.
- A fixed 60-second answer followed by a same-length retry matches 3/3/3 (constant time) more than 4/3/2. The evidence suggests a shorter retry (for example, 45 seconds) would emphasize fluency. That is an inference, not a tested design.

### Gaps
- Nation's original 1989 *System* paper and Maurice's 1983 source were not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis of 4/3/2 specifically was found.

## Pre-task planning and online planning

### Takeaway
Planning time before speaking reliably raises fluency, and usually complexity, in L2 speech. Accuracy gains are less consistent. Even 1 minute helped fluency and accuracy in Mehnert's study, while complexity needed about 10 minutes. Online planning (no time pressure while speaking) favours accuracy and complexity. A meta-analysis exists (Johnson & Abdi Tabari, *Applied Linguistics*), but I could not retrieve its effect sizes.

### Cited Findings
- **Mehnert (1998), *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 20, 52–83.** 31 learners of German, 4 groups: no planning, 1, 5 or 10 minutes, 2 tasks each. **Fluency and lexical density rose as planning time grew. Accuracy improved with only 1 minute and did not increase with more time. Complexity was significantly higher only with 10 minutes.** No difference between tasks — [Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-different-lengths-of-time-for-planning-on-second-language-performance/B828DF63B5C5ADAA2E8CCD1A03870575)
- Reviews list Crookes (1989), Foster & Skehan (1996), Skehan & Foster (1997), Wendel (1997) and Mehnert (1998) as finding that pre-task planning increases fluency. Effects on hesitation and repair specifically are described as mixed (per search summary of secondary reviews; source page not checked; the Mehnert link above is for Mehnert only)
- **Yuan & Ellis (2003)** (journal not confirmed in what I retrieved; usually cited as *Applied Linguistics* 24(1)): pre-task planning improved grammatical complexity, and pre-task planners were more fluent and lexically varied than online planners. **Online planning** (speaking without time pressure, so the speaker can formulate and monitor) improved accuracy and complexity (per search summary of secondary descriptions; the source page for this abstract was not confirmed) — see also [Ellis & Yuan 2004, written version, SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/46C3D44F045A0334DC671F9E0030BA5A)
- **Ellis (2009), "The differential effects of three types of task planning on the fluency, complexity, and accuracy in L2 oral production", *Applied Linguistics* 30(4), 474–509** (a review across studies) — [Scinapse listing](https://scinapse.io/papers/2070580370)
- **Meta-analysis:** Johnson & Abdi Tabari, "Task planning and oral L2 production: A research synthesis and meta-analysis", *Applied Linguistics*. It compares planning effects on syntactic complexity, accuracy, lexical complexity and fluency. Effect sizes were not available in the sources I retrieved — [ECU news item](https://english.ecu.edu/?p=13678)
- A separate Chinese-language meta-analysis exists: Geng & Li (2025), "The role of task planning in L2 oral production", *Modern Foreign Languages* 48(1), 103–115 — [PolyU listing](https://www.polyu.edu.hk/fh/research/research-output/journal-articles/the-role-of-task-planning-in-l2-oral-production/)

### Inferences
- A short planning window (around 30–60 seconds) is supported for fluency and accuracy by Mehnert's 1-minute condition, which is one study of 31 people. Gains in complexity likely need longer than an app would give.
- For native speakers, planning time mainly helps *conceptualization* (what to say and in what order), so it plausibly affects "clear point" and structure more than word-level fluency. This is untested.
- There is a design tension: the app trains *unprepared* speaking. Planning time raises performance on that attempt, but there is no evidence it builds the skill of speaking without preparation. One option is planning time on the first attempt and none on the retry.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes from the Johnson & Abdi Tabari meta-analysis, and whether planning times under 1 minute were analysed as a moderator.
- No study found that tests exactly 30 seconds of planning.
- No native-speaker planning study was found in this search.

## Feedback after speaking: corrective feedback, self-transcription and self-review

### Takeaway
Corrective feedback has meta-analytic support for improving L2 *accuracy*, with durable effects, and prompts (pushing the speaker to fix it) beat recasts (the listener just says it correctly). This literature is about grammar and pronunciation errors, not fluency or organization, so it transfers only indirectly to native speakers. Self-transcription (transcribing and reviewing your own recording) rests on small classroom studies, and one study found learners noticed only 25% of their own errors.

### Cited Findings
- **Lyster & Saito (2010), "Oral feedback in classroom SLA: A meta-analysis", *SSLA* 32, 265–302.** 15 classroom studies, N = 827. Corrective feedback "had significant and durable effects on target language development." **Effects were larger for prompts than for recasts**, especially on free, open-ended production measures — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- Earlier meta-analyses, as cited in Lyster & Saito (2010): Russell & Spada (2006) d = 1.16; Mackey & Goo (2007) d = 0.71 at immediate post-test and d = 1.09 at delayed post-test (per search summary of Lyster & Saito's introduction; not checked against the full text) — [Lyster & Saito, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- **Li (2010), "The effectiveness of corrective feedback in SLA: A meta-analysis", *Language Learning* 60(2), 309–365:** an overall medium effect, according to a reading-list annotation (per search summary; source page not checked). The exact d is unverified — DOI [10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x)
- **Self-transcription:** Lynch (2001), "Seeing what they meant: transcribing as a route to learning", *ELT Journal* 55(2), 124–132; Lynch (2007), "Learning from the transcripts of an oral communication task", *ELT Journal* 61(4); Mennim (2003), "Rehearsed oral L2 output and reactive focus on form", *ELT Journal* 57(2), 130–138. They argue that transcribing your own speech after the task lets you notice problems you can't attend to while speaking in real time. These are small classroom studies; methods and effect sizes were not verified — [reference list and summary](https://revistas.udistrital.edu.co/index.php/calj/article/view/4989)
- **Counter-evidence:** Salas (2015, *Colombian Applied Linguistics Journal*), 20 English-teaching trainees in Chile transcribed and coded 3 minutes of their own speaking test. They noticed only about **25% of their errors**, and the results were "much less positive" than the earlier literature suggested — [Colombian Applied Linguistics Journal](https://revistas.udistrital.edu.co/index.php/calj/article/view/4989)

### Inferences
- The finding that prompts beat recasts maps onto "tell them the one thing, then have them try again" more than onto "just show the better version." A better version is closer to a recast (the correct form, given to you). Pairing it with a retry turns it into a prompt-plus-practice cycle. This is an analogy, not a tested equivalence.
- Unaided self-review appears weak: people miss most of their own problems. A transcript with the issues marked (counted fillers, the point located) is likely more useful than replay alone. This is inferred from Salas (2015) and the noticing rationale.
- None of these meta-analyses measure fluency or organization; they measure grammatical accuracy. Their direct relevance to native speakers is low.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes for Lyster & Saito (2010) and Li (2010).
- No controlled study found on recorded self-review (watching or listening back) for native-speaker impromptu speech.
- Lynch's and Mennim's original sample sizes and results were not verified.

## Deliberate practice, spacing and frequency

### Takeaway
Deliberate practice explains a modest share of skill differences, much less in education and the professions than early claims suggested (Macnamara et al. 2014). Spacing practice out beats massed practice in L2 learning in general (meta-analysis of 37 studies), but for task repetition, longer gaps between repeats went with smaller *immediate* fluency gains. No study applies Ericsson-style deliberate practice to impromptu speaking directly.

### Cited Findings
- **Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), *Psychological Science*.** Meta-analysis, 157 effect sizes, more than 11,000 participants. Deliberate practice explained **26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions**: "important, but not as important as has been argued." Ericsson disputed how deliberate practice was defined — [PMC "Facing facts about deliberate practice"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4101876/); [summary](https://stafforini.com/works/macnamara-2014-deliberate-practice-and/)
- **Kim & Webb (2022), "The effects of spaced practice on second language learning: A meta-analysis", *Language Learning* 72(1), 269–319.** 37 experimental studies. Spacing benefits L2 learning overall, though the effect differs across language areas, learners and activities, and some subgroups had too few studies to conclude anything — [Serrano 2022 review in SSLLT](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602)
- Task repetition meta-analysis: longer intervals between repetitions were associated with smaller effects on fluency and syntactic complexity — [Hildesheim](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19120)

### Inferences
- The core elements of deliberate practice (a specific goal, immediate feedback, repeating the weak part) are present in the task-repetition-plus-feedback studies above. Those studies are better direct evidence for speaking than Ericsson's general framework.
- The two spacing findings point in different directions but fit together: an immediate retry maximises same-day fluency gains, while spreading practice across days likely helps retention (from general spacing research). A daily habit with a same-session retry covers both. No speaking study tested this combination.

### Gaps
- No study of how often or how long to practice impromptu speaking. No dose-response data for speaking fluency was found.

## What listeners hear as fluent: speech rate, pause location, repairs and mean length of run

### Takeaway
A meta-analysis (Suzuki, Kormos & Uchihara 2021) shows that listeners' fluency judgments follow measurable timing features closely. The strongest are overall speech rate (r ≈ .76) and **pauses in the middle of a clause (r ≈ −.72)**. Pauses between clauses matter much less (r ≈ −.48), and repairs matter least (r ≈ −.20). For native speakers, filled pauses ("um") are noticed mainly when listeners attend to style; no pauses gives the best impression, but "um" is not judged worse than silent pauses.

### Cited Findings
- **Suzuki, Kormos & Uchihara (2021), "The relationship between utterance and perceived fluency: A meta-analysis of correlational studies", *Modern Language Journal* 105(2), 435–463.** Correlations with perceived fluency, as reported in a later Cambridge paper citing it:
  - speech rate r = .76; articulation rate (speed while actually talking) r = .62
  - silent pause frequency r = −.59; **mid-clause pause frequency r = −.72; between-clause pause frequency r = −.48**; pause duration r = −.46
  - disfluency or repair rate r = −.20 (significant but weak)
  — [Cambridge article citing the meta-analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FAB18835B2117A299C33F8F502248C81); [Waseda listing of the meta-analysis](https://w-rdb.waseda.jp/html/100003147_en.html)
- In that later study (102 Japanese speakers of English, 10 experienced raters), timing measures explained 61% of the variance in fluency ratings. **The mid-clause pause ratio was the strongest single predictor (R² = .449)**; speed and repair added nothing significant. Multi-word phrase use added only 0.8% — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FAB18835B2117A299C33F8F502248C81)
- Lambert et al. (2017): repetition reduces between-clause pauses first, then mid-clause pauses — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/task-repetition-and-second-language-speech-processing/0EA95A4C7D9E90CD2AB30043F84A4635)
- **Native speakers: Christenfeld (1995), "Does it hurt to say um?", *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior* 19, 171–186.** Listeners *assume* "um" signals anxiety or poor preparation, though it did not appear to come from either. How many ums listeners noticed depended on what they were attending to: few when focused on content, most when focused on style. **Filled pauses made a better impression than silent pauses, and no pauses was best.** Ums affected ratings even when listeners couldn't report hearing them. Sample sizes were not verified — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02175503); [UCSD PDF](https://library.ucsd.edu/dc/object/bb1571233s/_2.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Where you pause matters more than whether you pause.** Pausing at a clause or sentence boundary sounds like planning; pausing mid-phrase sounds like struggle. This is one of the best-supported, most transferable findings for an app: it is about listener perception, which applies to any speaker, though the underlying ratings are mostly of L2 speech.
- Counting fillers alone may over-weight a weak cue. Christenfeld suggests ums are a smaller problem than people think, and replacing "um" with a silent mid-clause pause would not help. Coaching should aim at fewer pauses in the middle of a clause, not just fewer ums, and "pause instead of um" is best framed as a pause at a sentence boundary.
- Repairs and restarts are the weakest correlate of perceived fluency (r ≈ −.20).

### Gaps
- De Jong's, Kormos & Dénes (2004), Segalowitz's and Tavakoli's primary papers were not retrieved; mean length of run is not reported here with a sourced number.
- Native-listener ratings of *native* speakers' fluency or articulateness on these timing measures were not found beyond Christenfeld.
- No sourced evidence links timing measures to perceived *clarity* or *organization* (as opposed to fluency).

## Thinking before speaking, retrieval and "verbal fluency" training in native speakers

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed trials showing that native speakers' impromptu articulateness improves with retrieval or "verbal fluency" training. Treat this as an evidence gap.

### Cited Findings
- None retrieved. Planning-time research (Mehnert 1998; Yuan & Ellis 2003) is L2-only — [Mehnert](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-different-lengths-of-time-for-planning-on-second-language-performance/B828DF63B5C5ADAA2E8CCD1A03870575)

### Inferences
- The closest proxies are task repetition and planning, which act on conceptualization and formulation, stages that native speakers share. The claim that they help native speakers is plausible but unproven.

### Gaps
- "Verbal fluency" in psychology usually means clinical word-generation tests (for example, naming as many animals as possible in a minute), not discourse fluency. Training studies of that kind were not searched in depth.
- No studies were found on native-speaker impromptu speaking (e.g., debate or Toastmasters-style table topics) with measured outcomes.

## Does practicing impromptu speaking reduce speaking anxiety?

### Takeaway
Yes, for fear of public speaking in general. A meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials finds medium-to-large effects that grow at follow-up. Most of the treatments were exposure, cognitive or behavioural methods, run by therapists or instructors, not app practice. Study quality is mixed, and effects are smaller on behavioural or physiological measures than on self-report.

### Cited Findings
- **Ebrahimi et al. (2019), "Psychological interventions for the fear of public speaking: A meta-analysis", *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:488.** 30 RCTs, 1,355 participants, 62 interventions.
  - Fear of public speaking: **g = 0.74 after treatment** (95% CI 0.61–0.87, number needed to treat 2.5) and **g = 1.11 at follow-up** (0.90–1.31; 11 studies)
  - General social anxiety: g = 0.35 after treatment and g = 0.70 at follow-up
  - Larger against waitlist controls (g = 0.82) than placebo controls (g = 0.65)
  - Technology-delivered treatments (VR, internet, computer) g = 0.66 vs face-to-face g = 0.75, not significantly different, but only 7 technology-delivered interventions
  - 50 of 62 interventions were cognitive or behavioural (exposure, cognitive restructuring, relaxation); 9 were systematic desensitization
  - Quality: only 1 of 30 studies used intention-to-treat analysis, allocation concealment was unclear in 28, there were signs of publication bias (trim-and-fill adjustment was small), and effects were smaller on physiological and behavioural measures
  — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6428748/)
- **McWilliam, Beattie & Callow (2025), "Review and analysis of successful PSA interventions: An applied perspective", *The Journal of Psychology* (systematic review and meta-analysis, studies from 2000 to June 2023):** 26 studies, 2,253 participants, 42 interventions, overall **g = 1.17** (95% CI 0.88–1.45), with high heterogeneity. The authors rate the included studies as moderate to high in quality, and call for more rigorous research on long-term and real-world effects. Read from the abstract only — [Bangor University record](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/review-and-analysis-of-successful-psa-interventions-an-applied-pe/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39970240/)
- **Allen (1987), Michigan State dissertation** (groundwork for Allen, Hunter & Donohue 1989, *Communication Education*): 181 experiments on reducing public speaking anxiety, grouped as systematic desensitization, cognitive modification, skills training, or combinations. **The treatments' effects were additive** (combinations did better) — [MSU dissertation](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/28955/OBJ/download)

### Inferences
- Repeated, graded speaking practice is a form of exposure, so lower anxiety from daily practice is plausible. However, the trials mostly tested structured therapy or instructor-led treatments for people with high anxiety, often with an audience. Speaking alone to a phone is a weak exposure; effects in an app are likely smaller than the pooled g.
- Allen's finding that treatments add up suggests that pairing practice (exposure) with skills feedback should beat either alone. This is consistent with the app's design, but untested in an app.
- Technology delivery was not significantly worse than face-to-face, but it rests on only 7 interventions.

### Gaps
- No trial found of solo, app-based impromptu speaking practice that measured anxiety.
- Allen, Hunter & Donohue (1989) effect sizes were not verified.
- Whether a lower *L2* speaking anxiety (foreign language anxiety) result applies to native speakers was not researched.

## Overall evidence grading (summary for the report writer)

A roll-up of the sections above. No new claims; the sources are cited in each section.

- **Strong (meta-analyses, replicated):** task repetition improves L2 speaking, with smaller effects on fluency than on complexity and accuracy (Abdi Tabari et al. 2025). Listener fluency judgments follow speech rate and mid-clause pauses most closely (Suzuki et al. 2021). Corrective feedback improves L2 accuracy durably, and prompts beat recasts (Lyster & Saito 2010; Li 2010). Psychological interventions reduce fear of public speaking, g ≈ 0.74 post and 1.11 at follow-up (Ebrahimi et al. 2019); g = 1.17 in a 2025 meta-analysis with high heterogeneity (McWilliam et al. 2025). Spacing helps L2 learning overall (Kim & Webb 2022). Deliberate practice explains little variance outside games, music and sports (Macnamara et al. 2014).
- **Moderate (several primary studies, consistent):** pre-task planning raises fluency (Mehnert 1998; Foster & Skehan 1996; Yuan & Ellis 2003); a planning meta-analysis exists but its numbers were not retrieved.
- **Weak or single-study:** transfer of repetition gains to new topics (de Jong & Perfetti 2011, n = 24); 4/3/2 shrinking time beats constant time for fluency (Thai & Boers 2016, n = 20); 1 minute of planning is enough for accuracy (Mehnert 1998, n = 31); self-transcription helps (Lynch, Mennim; contradicted by Salas 2015, n = 20); um vs silent pauses in native speech (Christenfeld 1995).
- **No evidence found:** native-speaker training trials for impromptu articulateness; app-based solo practice for anxiety; how often or how long to practice speaking.
