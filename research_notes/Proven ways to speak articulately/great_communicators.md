# How admired communicators speak clearly when unscripted, and what listener research says makes spoken explanations clear

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[EVIDENCE]** controlled study or peer-reviewed analysis (strength noted)
- **[PRACTICE]** codified institutional practice (military, consulting, Amazon). Widely used, not tested experimentally for speech.
- **[ANECDOTAL]** speechwriter advice, profiles, observation of individual communicators

Note on sourcing: most academic findings below were checked through abstracts, publisher records or citation summaries, not full texts. Where only a secondary summary was found, that is said.

---

## 1. What patterns do analysts point out in unscripted speech of admired communicators (Obama, Feynman, Jobs, Buffett, Ardern, Nadella, interviewers)?

### Takeaway
Rigorous linguistic analyses of specific admired speakers' *unscripted* speech are scarce. The best-documented case (Obama) shows that an admired speaker can use a lot of "uh"/"um" in unscripted speech: his clarity comes from structure and plain words, not from zero fillers. Most of what is said about Feynman, Jobs, Buffett and others is anecdotal.

### Cited Findings
- **Obama uses many filled pauses in unscripted speech. [EVIDENCE, descriptive counts]** Linguist Mark Liberman's tally found an "uh" or "um" roughly every 19 words in one Obama interview, against as rarely as once every 117 words for Donald Trump; another count found nine "uh"s in two minutes of a 2012 debate. The linguist writing the piece argues filled pauses often aid understanding and are not evidence of incompetence. — [The Conversation, "Presidential pauses"](https://theconversation.edu.au/presidential-pauses-what-those-ums-and-uhs-really-tell-us-about-candidates-for-the-white-house-204972)
- **Obama's G-20 press conferences were studied by splitting prepared remarks from the Q&A (treated as spontaneous speech).** Vocabulary variation was similar across speeches; a related study compared pausing in read vs. spontaneous parts of the London speech (findings not visible in the abstract). — [Genç, Göksu & Büyükkarcı (abstract)](https://abakus.inonu.edu.tr/items/23fde2a5-d96d-4842-ac27-34f45dbb7293/full)
- **Obama's speechwriter on plain language. [ANECDOTAL]** Jon Favreau described the speechwriter's job as explaining simple policies "in as few words as possible." — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2013/03/reading-the-presidents-mind)
- **Summarize the point in a few conversational sentences before speaking; cut what doesn't support it. [ANECDOTAL]** Favreau on the Obama speech process. — [Favreau, "Three lessons in storytelling" (PDF)](https://demofinland.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Three-lessons-in-storytelling_Jon-Favreau-English.pdf)
- **"Waste no words. Tell a story."** Favreau: the best speeches are built on a narrative arc, not a collection of snappy lines. [ANECDOTAL] — [Ragan](https://www.ragan.com/?p=48063); [Holy Cross Magazine](https://magazine.holycross.edu/stories/holy-cross-alumnus-and-obamas-former-speech-writer-jon-favreau-03-offers-tips-oscar-winners)
- **Feynman's explanations ("Fun to Imagine", BBC 1983) start from everyday phenomena** (fire, magnets, rubber bands, train wheels). [ANECDOTAL/descriptive] — [Open Culture](https://openculture.com/2024/12/richard-feynman-enthusiastically-explains-how-to-think-like-a-physicist-in-fun-to-imagine.html)
- **Feynman refused a misleading analogy.** In the magnets segment he declined to explain magnetism with rubber bands because the analogy would be circular; an essay contrasts this with Hawking's heavy use of everyday analogies. Lesson: an analogy must be accurate, not just familiar. [ANECDOTAL] — [U of Toronto essay](https://hep.physics.utoronto.ca/~pekka/pmu199y201718/ExampleEssay1.docx); [Spodek](https://joshuaspodek.com/fun-to-imagine)
- **The "Feynman technique" (explain it simply, find gaps, simplify, use analogies) is a later label,** not a method Feynman himself published; attribution should be treated with caution. — [Sketchplanations](https://sketchplanations.com/feynman-learning-technique)

### Inferences
- For the app: Obama's case is a useful caution against treating filler count as the main clarity measure. A speaker can be judged articulate with a moderate filler rate if the answer has a clear point, plain words and structure.
- "Can you say your point in one or two conversational sentences?" (Favreau) maps directly to the app's existing "clear main point" mark.
- Analogies help only when accurate; an app can encourage a concrete example but can't easily judge analogy quality.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed linguistic analyses were found of unscripted speech by Steve Jobs, Warren Buffett, Jacinda Ardern, Satya Nadella, Michelle Obama, or named podcasters/interviewers. Claims about them (e.g., Jobs' "rule of three", Buffett's plain-English shareholder letters) exist in popular books and blogs, but nothing reliable was found within this search budget. Peggy Noonan's advice was not checked.
- Favreau's specific advice on short sentences could not be found in his own words.

## 2. Business communication cultures (BLUF, McKinsey answer first, Amazon narratives, inverted pyramid): how do they translate to speech?

### Takeaway
All of these put the conclusion first and support beneath it. They are **codified practice, not experimentally tested for spoken answers**. Translated to a 60-second answer: say the point in the first sentence or two, then one reason or example, then close.

### Cited Findings
- **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front) comes from U.S. Army Regulation 25-50** on correspondence: open with the main point, a short purpose sentence, then the recommendation or key information; active voice. Also in Air Force *Tongue and Quill*. [PRACTICE] — [Wikipedia: BLUF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication)); [Matt Strom, "BLUF"](https://matthewstrom.com/writing/bluf)
- **Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto, McKinsey):** conclusion at the top, supporting arguments below, detailed evidence below those. [PRACTICE] — [untools: Minto Pyramid](https://untools.co/minto-pyramid/)
- **Amazon: PowerPoint banned from executive meetings by Bezos' email of June 9, 2004;** he argued the narrative structure of a memo "forces better thought." The 2017 shareholder letter: "We don't do PowerPoint ... presentations at Amazon"; memos are read silently at the start of meetings, and their quality varies widely. [PRACTICE] — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/04/23/what-jeff-bezos-learned-from-requiring-6-page-memos-at-amazon.html); [Researchscape on the shareholder letter](https://researchscape.com/blog/bezos-on-narratives)

### Inferences
- Amazon's lesson is the opposite of "answer first": it favours connected reasoning (full sentences with "because" and "so") over bullet fragments. In speech, both apply: lead with the point (BLUF/Minto), then connect the reason to it with explicit links rather than listing loose fragments.
- Detectable in a transcript: time (seconds) or word index at which the main claim first appears; presence of a causal connective ("because", "so", "which means") linking reason to point; whether the last sentence returns to the point.
- The newsroom inverted pyramid was not separately researched; it is the same "most important first" principle.

### Gaps
- No experimental study was found testing whether answer-first *spoken* answers are rated clearer or better remembered than build-up answers. The case rests on practice and on the listener-memory reasoning in section 3. This is the biggest evidence gap for the app's "say it in your first sentence" tip.

## 3. Listener research: signposting, fillers, hedges/powerless language, concreteness, pausing and rate, curse of knowledge

### Takeaway
Best supported: **signposting/structure markers help comprehension**, **concrete language is understood and recalled better**, and **hedges plus hesitations lower perceived authority**. Filler research is genuinely mixed: fillers help listeners process speech moment to moment, but at high rates they hurt how competent the speaker seems. Repetitions are cheap for listeners; false starts are costlier. No single optimal speaking rate is supported by good evidence.

### Cited Findings

**Signposting / discourse markers**
- **Chaudron & Richards (1986), *Applied Linguistics* 7(2):** lectures in four versions (no markers, micro, macro, both). Macro-markers (signalling structure: "the first point is", "now let's turn to") aided recall more than micro-markers ("so", "well", "um"). [EVIDENCE, classic, L2 listeners; via secondary summaries] — [UEW thesis citing it](https://ir.uew.edu.gh/handle/123456789/1618); [Pasaa journal PDF](https://www.culi.chula.ac.th/Images/asset/pasaa_journal/file-11-108-gzu2qv631762.pdf)
- Students understood a lecture better with discourse markers than with them removed. [EVIDENCE, small EFL study] — [Asian EFL Journal](https://asian-efl-journal.com/main-editions-new/discourse-markers-in-academic-lectures/index.htm)
- Review: markers signalling lecture structure help comprehension; lecturers often under-signal phases. [EVIDENCE, review + small study] — [Martín del Pozo, Language Value](https://doaj.org/article/37f272a0ec5f48ac974cbf9523240d46)
- Caveat: nearly all this research is with second-language listeners in lectures; no large meta-analysis found.

**Filler words: the "helps listeners" side**
- **Clark & Fox Tree (2002), "Using uh and um in spontaneous speech," *Cognition* 84:73–111:** "uh" and "um" are conventional words; "uh" signals a short coming delay, "um" a longer one. Speakers use them to manage the conversation (holding the floor, signalling a search). [EVIDENCE, corpus analysis] — [Herbert Clark publications](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/pubs.html); [Wikipedia: Jean E. Fox Tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_E._Fox_Tree)
- **Fox Tree (2001):** fillers sped listeners' recognition of the following word (reported via secondary sources; original not checked). [EVIDENCE, secondhand] — [The American Scholar](https://theamericanscholar.org/?p=23086)
- **Arnold and colleagues (eye-tracking):** after a disfluent "thee uh...", listeners look toward an object not yet mentioned, treating the filler as a sign the speaker is searching. [EVIDENCE, via secondary] — [The American Scholar](https://theamericanscholar.org/?p=23086)
- **Brennan & Williams (1995):** listeners use fillers and pause length to judge how confident a speaker is in what they say (cited in a 2021 paper). [EVIDENCE, via secondary] — [ICNLSP 2021 paper](https://hal.telecom-paris.fr/hal-03577262/file/2021.icnlsp-1.27.pdf)

**Filler words: the "hurts the speaker" side**
- **Laske et al. (2024), *Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis*,** "Um, so, like, do speech disfluencies matter? A parametric evaluation": listeners rated speeches with 0, 5 or 12 filler sounds per minute; 12/min hurt ratings across most categories, 5/min did not significantly hurt perceived effectiveness. [EVIDENCE, but found only via a practitioner summary; sample size and stats unverified] — [Behaviorist Book Club summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/aba-fundamentals/um--so--like--do-speech-disfluencies-matter--a-parametric-evaluation-o/)
- Literature review: the dominant view frames fillers as hurting perceived communicative competence, but findings on perceived fluency remain inconclusive. [EVIDENCE, review] — [VNU Journal of Foreign Studies](https://jfs.ulis.vnu.edu.vn/index.php/fs/article/download/5428/4638)
- Fillers more accepted in informal settings, seen as unprofessional in formal ones. [EVIDENCE, weak questionnaire] — [NELT proceedings](https://proceedings.uin-alauddin.ac.id/index.php/naselt/NELT3/paper/download/1893/1475)
- A behaviour-analysis line of work trains speakers to reduce fillers via awareness training (Ortiz et al. 2022, JABA 55(1):230–248): reduction is trainable. [EVIDENCE, citation only] — [Behaviorist Book Club](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/aba-fundamentals/um--so--like--do-speech-disfluencies-matter--a-parametric-evaluation-o/)

**Repetitions and false starts (restarts)**
- **Fox Tree (1995), *Journal of Memory and Language*:** listeners processed sentences with a repetition ("the gift the gift department") about as quickly as fluent ones. [EVIDENCE; via profile + citation] That false starts *did* disrupt processing in the same paper is from background knowledge, not verified here; the gating and ERP studies below support it independently. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1006/jmla.1995.1032); [UCSC Review profile](https://review.ucsc.edu/winter.97/fox_tree.html)
- **Lickley & Bard (1996), gating study:** more words were never recognized in disfluent utterances; repetitions were easier to recover from than recasts (restarts that change the wording). [EVIDENCE] — [ISCA Archive ICSLP 1996](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_1996/lickley96_icslp.html)
- ERP study: false starts produced a P600-like (re-analysis) brain response; repetitions did not behave the same way. [EVIDENCE] — [McAllister et al., DiSS 2001](https://www.isca-archive.org/diss_2001/mcallister01_diss.html)

**Hedges and powerless language**
- **Hosman (1989), *Human Communication Research*:** hedges and hesitations each lowered perceived authoritativeness and sociability. [EVIDENCE] — [USM record](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16291)
- **Hosman & Wright (1987):** simulated courtroom: witnesses with low levels of hedges and hesitations were rated most authoritative and attractive; these cues also affected perceived guilt. [EVIDENCE] — [USM record](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16292)
- **Speaker expertise moderates** how hedges/hesitations affect impressions (Hosman et al., 1998). [EVIDENCE] — [USM record](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16286)
- Powerless language made witnesses less credible (Erickson et al. 1978); a meta-analysis (Burrell & Koper 1998) found powerless language tends to reduce persuasion. [EVIDENCE, via review] — [Durik et al. 2008 (PDF)](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%206/DurikETAL2008.pdf)
- Counterpoint: some hedges add accuracy ("appropriate fuzziness"); not all hedging is weak. [EVIDENCE, linguistics] — [John Benjamins chapter](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/books/9789027270054-pbns.248.07pis)

**Concreteness**
- **Sadoski and colleagues:** across four experiments with instructional text, concreteness (ease of imagery) was the variable most strongly related to rated comprehensibility and to recall, ahead of familiarity and interest. [EVIDENCE, reading not listening] — [search-result abstract; the hosting PDF's authorship/year were not confirmed](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/17727/bitstreams/63652/data.pdf) (likely Sadoski, Goetz & Fritz, 1993, *Journal of Educational Psychology*; unverified)
- Concrete nouns are recalled better than abstract ones, consistent with Paivio's dual coding theory; the dual-coding *explanation* is contested. [EVIDENCE] — [Richardson 2003, Open University](https://oro.open.ac.uk/927)

**Pausing, pause location and speech rate**
- **Bosker et al. (2013), *Language Testing* 30(2):159–175:** untrained listeners' fluency ratings of (L2) speech were best predicted by pauses and speed; repairs added little. Pause frequency and length both lowered fluency ratings. [EVIDENCE] — [MPI record](https://forms.mpi.nl/node/51306); [Utrecht portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/what-makes-speech-sound-fluent-the-contributions-of-pauses-speed-/)
- **Pause location matters:** a study in *Applied Psycholinguistics* tests how a pause's location (within vs. between clauses) affects perceived fluency. [EVIDENCE; only the title/record was seen, so the direction of the result is from background knowledge (mid-clause pauses judged less fluent) and must be checked in the paper] — [Cambridge: "The effect of pause location on perceived fluency"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/effect-of-pause-location-on-perceived-fluency/D8EF194FCD2C5FBF0D6BA49EF6F0624E)
- Speech rate: no rigorous universal optimum found. Comprehension of time-compressed speech holds up to roughly 275–300 wpm then drops (secondhand summary of Pastore & Ritzhaupt); slower speech helps mainly listeners with low working memory (secondhand). Common practitioner ranges (120–160 wpm) are unsourced. [WEAK] — [Rosetta Translation blog](https://www.rosettatranslation.com/how-fast-can-you-listen/); [Teleprompter blog](https://www.teleprompter.com/blog/why-your-pace-of-speech-matters)

**Curse of knowledge**
- **Elizabeth Newton (Stanford, 1990):** "tappers" tapped well-known songs; listeners identified 3 of 120 (2.5%) while tappers predicted 50%. Popularized by the Heaths in *Made to Stick* (2007) as the curse of knowledge: once you know something, it is hard to imagine not knowing it. [EVIDENCE, single dissertation study, widely cited] — [NPR interview with Chip Heath](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/7486659); [Heath & Heath, "The Curse of Knowledge" (PDF)](https://www.lectio.dk/lectio/265//lc/70391411001/res/70391411003/The%20Curse%20of%20Knowledge%20-%20Chip%20Heath%20and%20Dan%20Heath.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Fillers: report both sides.** Moment to moment, "um/uh" can help listeners; at high rates (around 12 a minute in one study) they hurt perceived competence, while around 5 a minute did not measurably hurt. A per-minute filler rate with a "low but not zero is fine" framing is better supported than "eliminate all fillers." Note that the app's filler list mixes true filled pauses (um, uh) with discourse markers (like, basically, actually, you know, sentence-initial "so"); the research above is mostly about um/uh, and markers like "so" can also function as signposts.
- **Restarts are worth flagging more than repetitions.** Repeating a word costs listeners little; abandoning and recasting a sentence costs more.
- **Pause location is more detectable and more meaningful than pause count.** From word timings an app can tell whether long silences fall between sentences (fine, even helpful) or mid-phrase (judged less fluent).
- **Hedge density** ("I think maybe", "kind of", "sort of", "I guess", tag questions like "right?") is countable from a transcript and has decent evidence of lowering perceived authority; but expertise and context moderate it, so it should be feedback, not a score.
- **Concreteness** could be approximated by counting concrete nouns, numbers, names and specific examples (e.g., against published concreteness norms), though the evidence is from reading, not listening.
- **Signposting** ("the main reason is", "for example", "so the bottom line") is detectable by phrase lists; the evidence for macro-markers is stronger than for micro-markers.
- Curse of knowledge suggests the "better version" rewrite should add plain-language framing for a listener who lacks context — but the app's rules forbid adding facts, so this is a tension to note, not a feature.

### Gaps
- Primary texts of Laske et al. 2024 and Fox Tree 2001 were not read; the 5-vs-12 per-minute threshold needs checking in the original before it is quoted to users.
- No good evidence found on an optimal English speech rate for comprehension of spontaneous explanations.
- No study found on concreteness effects in *listening* specifically (vs. reading).
- Most signposting and fluency research uses second-language listeners or second-language speakers; transfer to native speakers' everyday answers is assumed, not shown.

## 4. What do listeners rate as "articulate", "clear" or "competent" in spontaneous speech?

### Takeaway
In the perceived-fluency literature, the strongest predictors of listener ratings are **pauses (how many, how long, where) and speed**, not repairs. Credibility ratings drop with hedges, hesitations and high filler rates. Little research directly measures "articulate" in native spontaneous speech.

### Cited Findings
- Pause and speed measures best predicted untrained raters' fluency scores; raters were "most sensitive to the use of pauses." [EVIDENCE] — [Bosker et al. 2013, MPI](https://forms.mpi.nl/node/51306); [related MPI paper on acoustic measures and fluency ratings](https://www.mpi.nl/node/51370)
- Low levels of hedges and hesitations produced the most favourable authoritativeness ratings. [EVIDENCE] — [Hosman & Wright 1987](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16292)
- High filler rate (12/min) lowered ratings; low rate (5/min) did not. [EVIDENCE, unverified summary] — [Laske et al. 2024 summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/aba-fundamentals/um--so--like--do-speech-disfluencies-matter--a-parametric-evaluation-o/)
- Argument quality interacts with powerless features: tag questions and argument quality jointly affected perceived power and credibility. [EVIDENCE] — [Hosman & Siltanen 2011](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/437)

### Inferences
- Measurable proxies for "sounds articulate": long-pause rate and location, words per minute, fillers per minute, restarts, hedge count. All are plain-code measures from word timings, consistent with the app's rule of no AI for countable things.
- Hosman & Siltanen suggests content (argument quality) and delivery interact, which supports the app pairing delivery numbers with the judge's clear-point marks rather than reporting delivery alone.

### Gaps
- "Articulate" as a rated construct in native spontaneous speech was not found as its own research line; nearly all perceived-fluency work is on second-language speakers.

## 5. Feynman technique / explaining simply: is there evidence (self-explanation effect, Chi)?

### Takeaway
The "Feynman technique" itself has no direct experimental evidence and is a later label. Its closest evidence base is the **self-explanation effect**: prompting learners to explain material to themselves improves understanding. That is evidence about learning content, not about speaking more clearly to others.

### Cited Findings
- **Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994), *Cognitive Science* 18(3):439–477:** 14 eighth-graders prompted to self-explain each line of a text on the circulatory system gained more from pre- to post-test than a control group that read it twice; high explainers all reached the correct mental model, many low explainers and controls did not. [EVIDENCE, small sample, seminal] — [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/eliciting-self-explanations-improves-understanding/); [full text PDF](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/chiselfexplanation.pdf)
- Described as the first experimental intervention demonstrating the self-explanation effect; later work found prior knowledge strongly affects how well it works. — [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2020/2/20-1); [Cognitive Science Society paper](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/33432/galley/24491/download)
- Feynman technique as commonly described (explain simply, find gaps, simplify, analogies) is popular-press, attribution uncertain. [ANECDOTAL] — [Sketchplanations](https://sketchplanations.com/feynman-learning-technique)

### Inferences
- The app's core loop (answer aloud, see a tighter version, try again) resembles self-explanation plus feedback; this supports retry as a learning mechanism, but no study found tests self-explanation as a way to improve *spoken clarity*.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence found that the "Feynman technique" improves how clearly people speak.
