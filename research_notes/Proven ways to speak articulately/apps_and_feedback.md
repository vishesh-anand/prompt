# Speaking-practice apps and the evidence on automated / AI feedback for speaking

Research date: 2026-10-17. About 20 searches plus one full-text read (Shute 2008). Most app facts come from vendor pages, directories and press, not independent tests. Treat pricing as approximate: sources often disagree, and I could not open every official pricing page.

## 1. What current apps measure and teach (comparison)

### Takeaway
Nearly every app measures the same countable delivery features: filler words, pace, sometimes pauses, eye contact, "weak words" and repetition. Language-learning apps (ELSA, Speak, Stimuler) add pronunciation, grammar and vocabulary. Almost none assess content structure (a clear point, a reason, an ending), and none I found pairs a rewritten model answer with an immediate retry of the same question. Independent evidence on accuracy is close to nonexistent, and the user complaints I could find concern inaccurate scoring and slowness.

- Also flags "weak words" (e.g., "really", "basically", "kind of", "stuff") — [Yoodli blog](https://yoodli.ai/blog/yoodli-weekly-tip-be-deliberate-about-weak-words)

**Toastmasters (Pathways + club roles)** (human, not an app)
- The "Ah-Counter" role logs filler sounds ("ah", "um", "er"), overused words ("and", "but", "so", "you know") and long pauses for every speaker, and reports them in the evaluation part of the meeting — [Toastmasters: Ah-Counter](https://www.toastmasters.org/en-us/membership/club-meeting-roles/ah-counter)
- Toastmasters material urges going beyond "gotcha" tallying — [Toastmasters role library](https://content.toastmasters.org/image/upload/club-meeting-roles-resource-library.pdf)
- Pathways evaluators give a spoken and a written evaluation against project objectives, reinforcing strengths and "gently offering useful advice" — [Toastmasters Navigator](https://ccdn.toastmasters.org/medias/files/the-navigator/8722-the-navigator.pdf); [UCSF club](https://ucoracles.ucsf.edu/node/6)

### Inferences
- The countable metrics (fillers, pace) are commoditized. Every app has them, so they cannot set a new app apart. What sets one apart is content structure (clear point, reason, ending), one prioritized tip, a rewritten version, and an immediate retry with before-and-after numbers. I found no app doing all four.
- Google Interview Warmup's choice not to score at all, and Toastmasters' "beyond gotcha" guidance, both point the same way as the research in section 3: tallies alone do little, and next-step guidance matters.
- The main complaint pattern is accuracy ("inaccurate assessments"). A "This feedback is wrong" button and evals on transcription and judging address the most common failure users name.
- The market is moving to B2B roleplay (Yoodli, VirtualSpeech, Poised). Consumer daily-practice apps for native speakers who ramble are a thinner field.

### Gaps
- Current official pricing for Orai, Speeko, ELSA and Poised was not verified.
- I found no Reddit threads, and few App Store review samples, that specifically complain of shallow or wrong feedback for Yoodli, Orai, Poised or Speeko. A manual pass over r/publicspeaking and the App Store reviews is needed.
- No independent accuracy test of any app's filler counting or scoring was found.

## 2. Does app / AI feedback on speaking actually improve people?

### Takeaway
There is moderate evidence that automated feedback improves **pronunciation** (meta-analysis g = 0.69) and that dialogue-based systems help L2 speaking (g = 0.61). VR practice reliably **reduces anxiety**. Evidence that any app improves **fluency, structure or perceived quality for native speakers** is thin: mostly small HCI lab studies measuring user satisfaction or awareness, not lasting skill gains. App-specific studies (ELSA, Orai) are small, quasi-experimental and often uncontrolled.

### Cited Findings
**Measured outcomes (meta-analyses)**
- Automatic speech recognition (ASR) for ESL/EFL pronunciation: 15 studies, 38 effect sizes (2008–2021), overall Hedges' g = 0.69 (medium). Explicit corrective feedback was largely effective; indirect feedback (e.g., plain dictation) was only moderate. Effects were large for individual sounds and small for prosody (stress, intonation). Medium-to-long treatments beat short ones. Practising with peers had a large effect; practising alone a small one — [Ngo, Chen & Lai 2024, ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D/S0958344023000113a.pdf/the-effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Dialogue-based computer-assisted language learning for L2 speaking: three-level meta-analysis, moderate overall effect g = 0.61. Corrective feedback was **not** a significant moderator — [ReCALL 2025](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/31847710516602398819C5E594038E7B/S0958344025100268a.pdf/dialogue-based-computer-assisted-language-learning-systems-for-second-language-speaking-development-a-three-level-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Machine and human pronunciation scores correlate at only r = .606 (a validity measure, not a learning gain) — reported in the same search; [SAGE journal article, DOI 10.1177/02655322251387816](https://doi.org/10.1177/02655322251387816) (DOI resolves to journals.sagepub.com; journal title and figure not confirmed from the page)
- VR for public-speaking anxiety: meta-analysis of 92 publications found a significant reduction in anxiety. Effective protocols used about 6 sessions of about 37 minutes. VR performed about as well as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) — [Lim, Aryadoust & Esposito 2023, *Current Psychology* 42:12912–12928, DOI 10.1007/s12144-021-02684-6 (Trento repository record)](https://iris.unitn.it/handle/11572/360923). The "6 sessions, about 37 minutes" detail came from a search summary only, not the abstract record; verify
- Performance (not anxiety) effects of VR training are small-study and mixed. In one N=36 study, task-difficulty effects on performance became non-significant once other factors were controlled — [Poeschl 2017, Frontiers in ICT](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fict.2017.00013/pdf). A 3-session VR study (N=56) cut perceived anxiety and called for longer designs to test performance — [Valls-Ratés, UPF](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/4332bce9-f65f-47f0-a1dc-08c6cf6d7473/full)
- Meta-analysis of task repetition in oral production: repetition substantially improves syntactic complexity and accuracy; fluency also improves but less. Longer gaps between repetitions shrink the effects — [Hildesheim record](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19120). Bygate's original studies found fluency and complexity rose on the second telling — [Indrarathne, Lancaster](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/events/laelpgconference/papers/v08/Bimali-Indrarathne.pdf)

**HCI lab studies (Rochester ROC HCI group).** Note: the researcher is **M. Iftekhar Tanveer** (with Mohammed Ehsan Hoque), not "Saket Tanveer" as the brief says. Ummo's link to this group was not confirmed by any source found.
- Rhema (Google Glass, IUI 2015): 30 native English speakers each gave three 3-minute speeches (continuous feedback, sparse feedback, none). Participants were significantly more pleased with their speech under **sparse** feedback (p < .05) than under continuous or none. Short words every ~20 seconds ("louder", "slower", nothing when fine) were preferred over colours or graphs. Outcome was self-rated satisfaction, not audience-rated skill — [Tanveer, Lin & Hoque 2015 (PDF)](https://www.hoques.com/Publications/2015/2015-Rhema-IUI-Tanveer-Lin-Hoque.pdf); [Rochester news](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/wearable-technology-can-help-with-public-speaking-95552)
- AutoManner (IUI 2016): Kinect detected repetitive body movements and asked users what they meant instead of judging them. In 27 participants it raised awareness of mannerisms. Outcome was awareness, not behaviour change — [AutoManner PDF](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/automanner.pdf); [ROC HCI](https://roc-hci.com/past-projects/automanner/)
- ROC Speak (UbiComp 2015): semi-automated feedback on recorded videos (eye gaze, word use, voice level, gestures), later combined with human comments — [ROC HCI](https://roc-hci.com/past-projects/rocspeak/); [Microsoft Research blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/cloud-computing-changes-way-practice-public-speaking/)

**App-specific studies (weak designs)**
- ELSA Speak: several quasi-experiments (Indonesia, Vietnam, Egypt) with N = 9 to 71 all report pronunciation gains over comparison groups. Samples are small, there is no independent trial, and some statistics are poorly reported — [JELE](https://jele.or.id/index.php/jele/article/view/2073); [UIN Saizu thesis](https://repository.uinsaizu.ac.id/27586); [IREEL](https://jurnalfaktarbiyah.iainkediri.ac.id/index.php/ireel/article/view/6679)
- Orai: the only study found is a 24-student perception survey with no control group (students felt less afraid of speaking). There is no randomized trial — [JEELS](https://jurnalfaktarbiyah.iainkediri.ac.id/index.php/jeels/article/view/69)

**Marketing claims (not outcomes)**
- VirtualSpeech's "86% felt more confident" — [Stork](https://www.stork.ai/en/virtualspeech)
- Microsoft's link from fillers to perceived confidence is stated without a specific citation in its help text — [Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/powerpoint/suggestions-from-speaker-coach)

### Inferences
- The best-supported mechanisms are **explicit corrective feedback** (more than raw measurement) and **repetition of the same task** (fluency and structure improve on a second attempt, best with short gaps). That supports "feedback, then retry the same question right away."
- Practice "with peers" beat practice "alone" in the ASR meta-analysis. A solo app loses some effect; a coach-like voice may partly stand in for that, but this is untested.
- Gains on a retry are partly practice effects, since the second telling is easier anyway. Before-and-after numbers on a retry overstate real learning. The weekly trend across new questions is the more honest signal.
- No evidence was found that filler counting by itself improves speakers over time. Rhema measured satisfaction; AutoManner measured awareness.

### Gaps
- No randomized trial of Yoodli, Orai, Poised, Speeko or Speaker Coach measuring audience-rated speaking quality.
- No study of native speakers improving impromptu answer **structure** through automated feedback.
- The task-repetition meta-analysis's pooled fluency effect size was not retrieved.
- The Lim et al. 2023 VR meta-analysis: the full text was not read; the session-dose figures are unverified.

## 3. Feedback design research: what works best

### Takeaway
Feedback helps on average but often backfires: in Kluger & DeNisi, more than a third of feedback interventions **lowered** performance, mostly when feedback pulls attention to the self (praise, grades, comparisons with others) and away from the task. The best-supported designs: specific, task-focused feedback that says what to do next; feedback in small, manageable pieces (avoid overload); comparison with your own earlier attempts, not with other people; little or no praise; and feedback after the attempt, without interrupting. Immediate and delayed timing both work. Immediate helps short-term gains and hard tasks; delayed may help transfer.

### Cited Findings
- **Kluger & DeNisi 1996** (*Psychological Bulletin*): 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations. Average d = 0.41, but over one third of feedback interventions decreased performance, not explained by sampling error or by whether the feedback was positive or negative. Feedback intervention theory: effectiveness drops as attention moves toward the self and away from the task — [Kluger & DeNisi abstract](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc); [PDF copy](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf)
- **Hattie & Timperley 2007** (*Review of Educational Research* 77(1)): feedback answers three questions: where am I going (feed up), how am I going (feed back), where to next (feed forward). It works at four levels (task, process, self-regulation, self); self-level praise is least effective — [paper PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf); [Cengage summary](https://exploreinside.ngl.cengage.com/?p=3847)
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, "The Power of Feedback Revisited" (Frontiers in Psychology 10:3087): medium effect d = 0.48 (d = 0.55 before 35 extreme values were excluded); impact depends on the information content of the feedback — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
- **Shute 2008** (*Review of Educational Research*), "things to do" (Table 2, full text read) — [Shute 2008 PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf):
  - Focus feedback on the task, not the learner.
  - Give elaborated feedback (what, how, why), not just verification.
  - Present it in manageable units: too much at once can cause superficial learning and cognitive overload.
  - Keep feedback "as simple as possible but no simpler". Kulhavy et al. found overly complex feedback did not beat simpler feedback.
  - Be specific and clear; reduce uncertainty between performance and goal.
  - Give feedback after learners have attempted a solution.
  - Computer feedback is often taken as less biased than human feedback.
  - Section "Feedback Complexity and Length": if feedback is too long, learners ignore it, and lengthy feedback dilutes the message.
- **Shute 2008, "things to avoid" (Table 3):** normative comparisons with others; overall grades. Citing Wiliam: students given grades only showed no gains, comments only showed large gains, grades plus comments showed no gains. Also avoid praise ("sparingly, if at all"), feedback that threatens self-esteem, interrupting a learner who is mid-task, and extensive error diagnosis (costly, often inaccurate). Do not default to text only; consider audio or visual modes — [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- **Shute 2008, self-referenced vs normative:** low achievers given norm-referenced feedback attribute failure to ability and lose motivation. Self-referenced feedback (against your own past performance) raised expectations of future success and attributions to effort (McColskey & Leary 1985) — [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- **Timing (Shute 2008, Table 4):** immediate feedback gives faster short-term gains and suits difficult tasks; delayed feedback is linked to better transfer and suits simple tasks. Field studies tend to favour immediate, lab studies delayed — [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- **Timing in L2 speaking:** reviews find both immediate and delayed oral corrective feedback produce learning, with no consistent winner; the research base is thin — [Nassaji & Kartchava, Cambridge Handbook of Corrective Feedback](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-corrective-feedback-in-second-language-learning-and-teaching/corrective-feedback-timing-and-second-language-grammatical-development-research-theory-and-practice/B55C0024292324FBEC86F50C6375C669); a Persian EFL study found immediate and delayed both beat the control, with no difference between them — [JMRELS](https://jmrels.journals.ikiu.ac.ir/article_1769.html)
- **During-speech feedback:** in Rhema, sparse feedback (about every 20 s, one word) beat continuous feedback on user satisfaction — [Rhema PDF](https://www.hoques.com/Publications/2015/2015-Rhema-IUI-Tanveer-Lin-Hoque.pdf). Shute advises not interrupting a learner who is engaged in the task — [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- **Explicit vs indirect:** in ASR pronunciation training, explicit corrective feedback is more effective than indirect feedback such as dictation alone — [Ngo et al. 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D/S0958344023000113a.pdf/the-effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-meta-analysis.pdf)
- **Questions instead of verdicts:** AutoManner surfaced patterns and asked the user what they meant, which raised awareness — [AutoManner](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/automanner.pdf)

### Inferences
- "One tip, the single most important thing," fits Shute's manageable-units and simple-as-possible guidance and Hattie's feed-forward. Showing five metrics plus a tip plus a rewrite risks the overload Shute describes. Making the tip most prominent and the metrics secondary is the evidence-aligned layout.
- An overall score or grade per answer is the riskiest element by this literature (Wiliam's grades-plus-comments result). Showing component marks (clear point 0–6) is close to a grade, so pair it with the comment and keep it visually secondary.
- Before-and-after against your own first attempt and your starting point is self-referenced, the favoured form. Never compare users with each other (which is consistent with no leaderboards).
- "No generic praise; praise only specific improvement" matches Kluger & DeNisi and Shute on praise directing attention to the self.
- Feedback after the take, not during it, follows Shute's do-not-interrupt rule. Rhema shows that if in-speech cues are ever added, sparse one-word cues are the version to test.
- Self-assessment before feedback (predict your filler count, or say what your main point was) is not covered by the sources I retrieved. It is a plausible feed-up step but unsupported here.

### Gaps
- I found no study of how many metrics a speaking app should show; the overload argument comes from general formative-feedback reviews.
- No retrieved evidence on per-attempt goal setting for speaking, or on self-assessment before seeing automated speaking feedback.
- Hattie & Timperley's original effect sizes (often quoted as 0.70–0.79) were not verified in the paper itself; the 0.48 replication is better sourced.

## 4. Rewritten "better version" (model answer) vs pointing to specific moments

### Takeaway
No study I found tests "rewrite the learner's own spoken answer" against "point out specific moments" for speakers. The closest evidence is L2 corrective feedback on **recasts** (reformulating the learner's own utterance). Recasts can produce more noticing than prompts, but learners often fail to see them as corrections. Explicit feedback beats indirect. Shute warns against showing the answer before the learner has tried; showing it after an attempt, and then retrying, is the sequence the evidence supports.

### Cited Findings
- 105 Malaysian ESL learners: both recasts (reformulations) and prompts (cues to self-correct) led to noticing, but recasts produced higher noticing in every category, most of all "noticing the gap" between the learner's version and the target — [Choo et al. 2021, SiELE](https://jurnal.usk.ac.id/SiELE/article/view/18546)
- Recasts are often not seen as corrective. In text-chat studies (Lai & Zhao 2006; Sauro 2009) learners often failed to notice them, and spoken recasts can be heard as simple confirmation of meaning — [Asian EFL Journal](https://www.asian-efl-journal.com/main-editions-new/do-recasts-promote-noticing-the-gap-in-l2-learning/)
- Noticing varies by type: sound-level reformulations were noticed most, word choice next, grammar least — [Sato (NUS e-FLT)](https://e-flt.nus.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/sato-1.pdf)
- Explicit corrective feedback beats indirect feedback in ASR training — [Ngo et al. 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D/S0958344023000113a.pdf/the-effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Elaborated feedback (what, how, why) beats verification alone. Feedback should come after the learner's own attempt ("presearch availability" controlled studies show benefits) — [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- Task repetition on the same task improves complexity, accuracy and (less) fluency — [Hildesheim meta-analysis record](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19120)

### Inferences
- A rewritten version of the user's own answer works like a long-form recast: it maximizes "noticing the gap", but it is implicit about *what* changed. Pairing it with one explicit tip that names a real moment ("your point came at 40 seconds") covers both. This matches the recast and explicit-feedback findings together.
- Making the changes visible (e.g., showing where the main point moved to the front) would reduce the "recast not seen as a correction" problem. This is untested in the retrieved sources.
- Risk: a polished rewrite can lead users to memorize and recite it on the retry, inflating retry gains. The rewrite should stay close to the user's own content (which the project's "adds nothing" rule enforces).

### Gaps
- No study of model-answer or rewrite feedback for native-speaker impromptu speaking, or for spoken structure (as opposed to grammar).
- No head-to-head study of a rewritten answer vs time-stamped moment feedback.

## 5. Retention: what keeps people practising daily

### Takeaway
Hard evidence is scarce. Duolingo's streak is the reference design and is credited with retention gains in company and secondhand reports, but the one academic observational study suggests streak mechanics breed app dependency more than lasting habits. I found no retention data for speaking-coach apps. The abandonment of Ummo and the B2B pivot of others hint that consumer speaking apps struggle to retain users.

### Cited Findings
- Duolingo says the streak is designed with habit research to keep learners motivated, and Streak Freeze lets you pause for a day for flexibility (company source) — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Eight-week observational case study of Duolingo iOS: streak and related features encourage daily activity and lower the barrier to returning, but appear to foster app dependency rather than habits that run on their own — [Maaß, IJMAT](https://www.wr-publishing.org/index.php/ijmat/article/view/908)
- Secondhand write-ups of Duolingo experiments: a streak-goal screen improved retention weeks later even though the chosen goal was never stored (the commitment act itself seemed to matter); a "Perfect Streak Week" reward was also tested. Not peer-reviewed — [Lazyweb: streak goals](https://lazyweb.com/research/duolingo-streak-goals-retention); [Lazyweb: retention experiments](https://lazyweb.com/research/duolingo-retention-experiments)
- Widely quoted "20–40% DAU lift from streaks" figures have no primary source — [mwm.ai glossary](https://mwm.ai/glossary/daily-streak) (discount)
- Effective VR anxiety protocols used about 6 sessions of about 37 minutes, which suggests benefits need repeated sessions — [Lim et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/handle/11572/360923) (session detail unverified, see section 2)
- ASR pronunciation gains were larger with medium-to-long treatment duration — [Ngo et al. 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D/S0958344023000113a.pdf/the-effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Ummo appears abandoned (last update 2016, removed from the App Store) — [AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/software/ummo/about)

### Inferences
- A streak with a low daily minimum (one answer) is the best-supported cheap lever, but it measures app use, not skill. The day-3 / day-7 / week-3 return metric in PRODUCT.md is the right test.
- The "streak goal" result suggests a one-time commitment step during onboarding (for example, the reminder-time choice) may itself help retention.
- Since benefits need repeated, spaced sessions, short sessions (1–2 minutes) that lower friction suit the evidence on dose, but no study shows the right daily dose for impromptu speaking.

### Gaps
- No published retention or churn numbers for Yoodli, Orai, Speeko, Poised, ELSA or Stimuler.
- No controlled study of streaks (or Streak Freeze) in speaking apps.
- No evidence on whether visible progress charts or weekly coach notes affect retention.
