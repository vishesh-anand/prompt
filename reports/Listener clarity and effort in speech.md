# Make the listener's job easy, not just possible

Listeners judge an answer articulate when two things are true. They can say back the point, and getting there took little work. Research treats these as two separate measures. "Did the words get through" is called intelligibility. "How easy did it feel" is called comprehensibility, and it is the one that matters for you. Across 49 studies the two correlate only r = .57, and people show extra mental effort even when they understand every word. For fluent speakers, the features with the best evidence are about organization and hesitation, not polish. They are: whether the essential parts are there and in a sensible order, pauses in the middle of a clause, restarts, vague words like "thing" and "a lot", and hedges like "I guess". Long chains of "and" are a promising but weaker signal, from one study. Missing a point, a reason or an ending costs you more reliably than saying too much. The biggest problem with your list of ideas is that two of its headline measures, "seconds until the point" and "share of time that did no work", have never been tested against what listeners feel. No study has measured listening effort for organized against rambling speech from fluent native speakers. Nearly all the evidence comes from second-language speakers, older adults, clinical patients, lectures or writing. So the plan below keeps most of the ideas but changes their order. The friend eval comes first and decides which measure becomes the headline. The old three marks stay, because they are the best-supported part of the current app. The AI map shrinks to one headline line and four labels.

**How to read the evidence tags**

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | A meta-analysis, or many agreeing studies |
| **Moderate** | Several studies, or one solid experiment, that agree |
| **Weak** | One small study, a snippet, or studies that disagree |
| **None found** | The research looked and found nothing |
| **L2** | Second-language speakers only |
| **Clinical / ageing** | Built for aphasia, brain injury or older adults |
| **Written / lecture** | Tested on reading or long lectures, not short spoken answers |

## Understood and easy to follow are two different measures

The field's core framework comes from Munro and Derwing. They split listener judgements into three parts: intelligibility (what share of words the listener could write down), comprehensibility (a rating of how easy it was) and accent ([Munro & Derwing](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/bct.121.02mun)). A 2025 meta-analysis of 49 reports found that intelligibility and comprehensibility correlate **r = .57**. Speech can be fully understood and still be hard work ([Chau & Huensch 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BF27ACC694B94D3B99D8BC6D05BFCA54/S0272263125000014a.pdf/the-relationships-among-l2-fluency-intelligibility-comprehensibility-and-accentedness-a-meta-analysis.pdf)). (**Strong, L2.**) Your instinct that a rambling answer "can still be understood" matches this split exactly. For a fluent native speaker, intelligibility is nearly always perfect. Only comprehensibility is left to improve.

Effort is real even when understanding is complete. In a pupil-dilation study, listeners worked harder for accented speech *even when they recognized it accurately* ([McLaughlin & Van Engen 2020](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/task-evoked-pupil-response-for-accurately-recognized-accented-spe/)). (**Moderate**, about accent, not structure.) It also has a social cost. When identical talks were played at poor audio quality, listeners rated the researcher as less intelligent and less likable ([Newman & Schwarz 2018](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/good-sound-good-research-how-audio-quality-influences-perceptions/)). (**Weak–Moderate**: two experiments, about audio, not rambling.) Nobody has shown that a disorganized but understood answer lowers how competent the speaker seems. That step is plausible, but it is not proven.

"Effort" is not one number. Pupil size, reaction time and self-ratings of listening effort "rarely correlate with each other" ([Alhanbali et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01749/pdf)). (**Moderate.**) So the app has to pick one working definition. The field's practical default is a rated "how easy was this to follow" scale. That is the one to use with friends.

What drives comprehensibility changes with skill level. For beginners, it is pronunciation and vocabulary. **For advanced speakers, grammar and discourse-level features decide it** ([Applied Psycholinguistics, ability levels](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/second-language-speech-production-investigating-linguistic-correlates-of-comprehensibility-and-accentedness-for-learners-at-different-ability-levels/9944A1B88B900F5C65EEE9040DC2D7CD)). (**Moderate, L2.**) Extend that line to a fluent native speaker and organization is the main lever left. That fits your decision to leave pronunciation, grammar and vocabulary out of version 1.

The single most useful study is Tsunemoto and Trofimovich (2024). Twelve trained raters scored 60 one-minute spoken answers. Rated coherence correlated **r = .70** with ease of understanding. It stayed a predictor after accent was controlled for. What raised coherence was **the structure and order of the parts the task required**. What lowered comprehensibility was **additive connectives like "and"**: about 2.7 points lower on a 100-point scale for each extra one per 100 words. **Signposting words ("first", "second") predicted neither coherence nor comprehensibility** ([Tsunemoto & Trofimovich 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/coherence-and-comprehensibility-in-second-language-speakers-academic-speaking-performance/85221E116322A1A7ACD84242C454712A)). (**Weak–Moderate**: one well-run study, L2, academic tasks.)

One mismatch to settle first. You describe answers of up to 2 minutes, but PRODUCT.md says 60 seconds. Most of the evidence here comes from 45–60 second answers. One finding argues against going longer: in 60-second answers, people who said more words drifted further from the topic ([Hoffman et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6150697/)). Moving to 2 minutes would mean a PRODUCT.md change. It would also give the rambling problem more room.

## Organization and hesitation features have evidence; signposts and point-first do not

The table lists every feature with listener evidence that is relevant to a 1–2 minute answer from a fluent speaker. It says how researchers measured each one and whether code or AI can detect it.

| Feature | Listener evidence | How it was measured | Detect with | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Essential parts present, in a sensible order | Coherence r = .70 with ease of understanding; "task-essential parts in order" predicted coherence | 100-point rater sliders; hand-coded structure | AI judge with quotes | **Weak–Moderate, L2** |
| Missing parts (no point, no reason, no ending) | Under-description penalized far more than over-description | Listener ratings of instructions | AI judge | **Moderate** (lab, short phrases) |
| Pauses in the middle of a clause | Fluency ratings track mid-clause pauses (r = −.72) more than pauses between clauses (r = −.48) | Pause timing against clause boundaries | Code, from word timings | **Strong, L2** |
| Restarts (abandoned sentences), not simple repeats | Restarts make listeners miss words; repeats cost almost nothing | Word-detection and processing-speed tasks | Code | **Moderate** |
| Disfluency package ("mazes": false starts, tangles, "uh") | Lowered test scores and clarity ratings in matched lectures | 20-minute videotaped lessons, achievement tests | Code | **Moderate**, one lab, lecture |
| Vague words ("thing", "a lot", "kind of", "some") | r = −.59 and −.48 with learning; reportedly negative in 10 of 10 experiments (later review, not read directly) | Word list, rate per lecture | Code (word list) | **Moderate**, one lab, lecture |
| "And"-chaining | −2.7 points per extra connective per 100 words | Connectives per 100 words | Code | **Weak**, one L2 study |
| Hedges ("I guess", "sort of") | Lower perceived authority; powerless language reduces persuasion | Matched-message experiments | Code | **Moderate** |
| Off-topic stretches | Rated less focused and less clear on "how to" topics, not on stories | Hand-coded share of off-target content | AI judge | **Moderate, ageing** |
| Signposts ("first", "the main reason") | Predicted neither coherence nor comprehensibility in short answers; helped recall in lectures | Counts; lecture recall | Code | **Mixed**: L2 lectures yes, short answers no |
| Point first | **No spoken test.** In writing and news, no clear advantage; narrative order sometimes won | Comprehension and recall | AI judge | **None found** for speech |
| Extra unexplained detail | No effect on learning in the same experiment where vagueness and mazes hurt | Matched lectures | — | **Weak null** |

Here is the evidence behind each row, in plain terms.

**Pause location** is the strongest delivery finding. A meta-analysis of fluency ratings found speech rate (r = .76) and mid-clause pauses (r = −.72) matter most. Between-clause pauses matter less (r = −.48), and repairs least (r = −.20) ([Cambridge, citing Suzuki et al. 2021](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FAB18835B2117A299C33F8F502248C81)). In argued answers, mid-clause pause length and frequency also predicted *comprehensibility*, not just fluency ([Suzuki & Kormos 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/linguistic-dimensions-of-comprehensibility-and-perceived-fluency-an-investigation-of-complexity-accuracy-and-fluency-in-second-language-argumentative-speech-corrigendum/0E1DA47C1D2F30E35A56AF0FB8BD04B6)). (**Strong / Moderate, L2.**) One caveat: fluency and comprehensibility correlate r = .82, but that link weakens when different people rate them. Part of it is a halo effect ([Chau & Huensch 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BF27ACC694B94D3B99D8BC6D05BFCA54/S0272263125000014a.pdf/the-relationships-among-l2-fluency-intelligibility-comprehensibility-and-accentedness-a-meta-analysis.pdf)). Pause numbers and an effort rating are not two independent pieces of proof.

**Restarts versus repeats.** Listeners process a repeated word ("the gift the gift department") about as fast as fluent speech ([Fox Tree 1995](https://doi.org/10.1006/jmla.1995.1032)). Abandoning a sentence and starting over makes listeners miss more words ([Lickley & Bard 1996](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_1996/lickley96_icslp.html)). (**Moderate.**) The teacher-clarity research adds "mazes": false starts, halts, redundant words and tangles. In a 2×2×2 experiment with 150 students and 20-minute lessons, vagueness and mazes both lowered test scores. **Extra unexplained content did not** ([Land & Smith 1979](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002248717903000322)). When two lessons with identical content differed only in mazes and "uh"s, students rated the clean one clearer ([Benton & Li, IDEA Paper 83](https://ideacontent.blob.core.windows.net/content/sites/2/2021/05/Idea-Paper-83_5-21v4.pdf)). (**Moderate**, one lab, lectures.) A "5.1 mazes a minute" threshold circulates, but it could not be traced to a readable source. Don't use it.

**Vague words** are the best behaviour-level finding in the teacher-clarity literature, and they are new to your list. Hiller and colleagues counted words from nine categories in 15-minute lectures with matched content. A higher share of vague words went with lower learning: **r = −.59** in one set and **r = −.48** in another ([Fendick 1990](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)). Smith and Land's abstract says frequent vague terms lower achievement and make students rate the lesson as less effective ([Smith & Land 1981](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ258747)). A later article reports that their review found vague terms hurt learning in all 10 experiments, but that was seen only in a search snippet, not read directly. (**Moderate**, mostly one lab, 1970s–80s lectures, no independent replication.) Three categories overlap with what you already count. "Actually", "basically" and "you know" are in the "bluffing" category, which your filler list already covers. "Maybe", "probably" and "I guess" are hedges. The categories that are new are **ambiguous designation** ("thing", "stuff", "somehow"), **indeterminate quantity** ("a lot", "some", "a few", "various") and **multiplicity** ("kind of", "types of", "aspects"). These mark the places where a name, a number or an example was available but not given. The research measures density, not presence. "About 40%" is fine.

**Missing structure costs more than extra words.** When listeners rated instructions, they penalized under-description heavily (3.12 against 4.91 on a 5-point scale). They did not rate over-description as worse than concise speech ([Engelhardt et al. 2006](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Engelhardt-et-al.-2006_GriceanMaximQuantity_JML.pdf)). A later study found that listeners do penalize over-information once design flaws are removed ([Davies & Katsos 2013](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/78603/)). (**Moderate**, lab tasks with short phrases.) Either way, a missing part reliably hurts. Extra words hurt only sometimes.

**Off-topic stretches hurt in some answers but not in others.** In ageing research, listeners rated off-topic speakers as less focused, less clear and more talkative on **"how to" topics**. On personal stories they rated them the same ([Trunk & Abrams 2009](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19485651)). Older adults' longer, more off-target life stories were even rated as *better stories* ([James et al. 1998](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9793112)). People who were very off-target left conversation partners less satisfied ([Pushkar et al. 2000](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10879589)), and they got information across less efficiently ([Arbuckle et al. 2000](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10755290)). (**Moderate, ageing.**) The material that hurts is *irrelevant* material. Material that is indirectly relevant, such as causes, feelings and examples, does not. Your Work questions resemble "how to" answers, where concision is rewarded. Everyday questions resemble stories, where some elaboration is welcome.

**Some features do not hold up.** Signposting predicted nothing in short answers (Tsunemoto & Trofimovich, above). Big-picture signposts did help recall in long lectures for second-language listeners. In audio, a preview sentence helped listeners outline content, but headings did not ([Lorch, Chen & Lemarié 2012](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/communicating-headings-and-preview-sentences-in-text-and-speech/)). (**Moderate**, synthetic voice, expository text.) Examples have surprisingly thin isolated support. In the teacher-clarity meta-analysis, "examples" was the weakest dimension (r = .23), and it mostly meant worked classwork examples ([Fendick 1990](https://archive.org/download/correlationbetwe00fend/correlationbetwe00fend_djvu.txt)). A deliberate closing recap that restates the point has some support. In one weak study, an *informative* summary helped retention and a summary that only named the topics did not ([Leiden thesis](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3185883/view)). (**Weak.**)

**"Say your point first" remains untested in speech.** The classic "disorganized speech" experiments shuffled sentences at random. A scrambled speech lowered persuasion and the speaker's perceived authority. In the same study, **disfluency hurt credibility more broadly than disorganization did** ([McCroskey & Mehrley](http://www.jamescmccroskey.com/publications/39.htm)). (**Moderate**, 1960s students, one scripted speech.) Those studies say nothing about point-early versus point-late. In writing, deductive and inductive order gave the same comprehension, and the preference varied by culture ([Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/inductively-versus-deductively-structured-product-descriptions-ef/)). In news, the inverted pyramid gave only a marginal comprehension edge ([Sternadori 2008](https://mospace.umsystem.edu/items/4f5c3c35-b2a9-4339-bc2f-4672afe32f88)). In TV news, it was the *least* effective structure for recall ([Benaissa Pedriza](https://revistascientificas.us.es/index.php/Comunicacion/article/download/21257/18729/85535)). (**Weak–Moderate, written / broadcast.**) The best guess at why point-first works is that it **gives the listener a frame early**. Deductive order as such is not the cause.

### The AI map: keep the headline, shrink the labels

The headline line ("Your point came at 1:00; in the clear version it comes first") is the strongest part of this idea. It names a real moment, which is what feedback research rewards ([Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)). The full side-by-side map is the weak part. Labelling every segment depends on first splitting the answer into units, and that step is shaky. Trained human raters agreed only **84%** on where units split ([Wright et al. 2014](https://talkbank.org/aphasia/discourse/lit/Wright2014.pdf)). On second-language spoken answers, humans agreed κ = .77 on which segment is central and which supports it, and κ = .65 on the relation between segments ([Wang et al. 2017](https://aclanthology.org/P17-2041/)). The best AI labelling spoken story structure reached κ = .79, against .87 between humans, and it did worse on adults' more complex speech ([Zhao et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.17205)). (**Weak–Moderate**: L2, Mandarin, an unreviewed preprint.) Expect agreement of κ 0.6–0.8 at best. Seven labels will do worse than four.

Use four labels: **point, support** (reason or example), **off-point or repeat**, and **ending**. "Hedge" and "false start" are word-level events, so code should count them. They should not be AI segment labels. A full map with two timelines also clashes with DESIGN.md ("very little on screen at once"). It clashes with the feedback rule to give feedback in small pieces, too ([Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)). Show the headline line and put the map behind a tap. No study shows that seeing a map of your answer helps you improve. One weak finding points the other way: people reviewing their own transcripts unaided found only **about 25% of their own errors** ([Salas 2015](https://revistas.udistrital.edu.co/index.php/calj/article/view/4989)). That supports pointing to one moment over handing over a full diagram.

### Seconds until the point: a good tip target, a risky headline

This number is concrete, countable once the point is found, and matches the coach voice. But it has four problems as *the* progress measure. First, no spoken experiment shows that an earlier point makes an answer easier to follow, and the written and news evidence is mixed (see above). Second, it depends entirely on the AI picking the right sentence as "the point". A wrong pick gives a confident, wrong number. Third, it is easy to game. A generic first sentence ("There are a few reasons") moves the number without helping the listener. Fourth, it suits Work answers better than Everyday stories, where extra talk was not penalized ([Trunk & Abrams 2009](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19485651)). Also, the retry inflates any measure, because the second attempt is partly rehearsal. Keep it as a tracked line and as the subject of the headline sentence. Promote it to *the* progress measure only if the friend eval shows that answers with an earlier point were rated easier to follow.

### "Time that did no work": narrow it, and don't count set-up

The research version of this idea exists, but only in clinical and ageing work. Aphasia research scores each word as carrying relevant information or not ("Correct Information Units") ([Nicholas & Brookshire 1993](https://utdanningsforskning.no/referanser2/2017/november/21/nicholas-l.-e.--brookshire-r.-h.-1993.-a-system-for-quantifying-the-informativeness-and-efficiency)). Healthy speakers probably score near the top, so it would not separate a tight answer from a circling one. Its reliability also collapsed in open conversation: human raters agreed at most **63%** ([Oelschlaeger & Thorne 1999](https://talkbank.org/aphasia/access/English/NonProtocol/0docs/oelschlaeger1999b.pdf)). AI models doing this task **over-label words as useful** ([arXiv 2606.15696](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.15696)). That means an AI judge will under-report wasted time. Ageing research uses a simpler 0–9 scale for the share of off-target content, and three coders agreed at 0.89 ([Yin & Peng 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4844921/)). (**Moderate, ageing.**) That scale is a better model than a precise percentage.

Two changes. **Don't count set-up before the point as waste.** It double-penalizes what seconds-to-point already measures, and context before a story can help (narrative order sometimes won on recall). **Don't penalize extra detail as such.** It had no effect on learning when vagueness and mazes did ([Land & Smith 1979](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002248717903000322)). Count three things: off-point stretches (irrelevant, not merely indirect), repeats of the point that add nothing (excluding a closing recap), and restarts. The first two need the AI, with quoted evidence. The third is code. Show it as one named stretch ("15 seconds on your commute didn't serve the question") rather than a percentage that looks more precise than it is.

### "Only when a listener would notice": let code decide

The evidence on what listeners notice is about **rate and location**, not individual events. Mid-clause pauses hurt more than between-clause ones. Restarts hurt more than repeats. Twelve fillers a minute hurt ratings and five or fewer did not, though that comes from a single study seen only through a summary ([Behaviorist Book Club summary of Laske et al. 2024](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/aba-fundamentals/um--so--like--do-speech-disfluencies-matter--a-parametric-evaluation-o/)). Check the paper before showing any threshold. Some "um"s even help listeners anticipate an unusual word ([Bosker 2015](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/321897/Bosker_DISS2015.pdf?sequence=1)). No study defines "noticeable", so an AI deciding it would invent a standard. It also goes against the project rule that anything countable is counted in code. Use code rules instead: flag mid-clause pauses over a set length, flag restarts but not simple repeats, and show fillers per minute framed as "low is fine". Treat hedges as a note, never a score. They lower perceived authority ([Hosman 1989](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16291); [Durik et al. 2008](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%206/DurikETAL2008.pdf)), but some hedges are simply honest.

### Pause location: keep

This has the best evidence on your list (see the table). Your word timings make it plain code. The weak point is finding clause boundaries. Transcript punctuation is an approximation, so test the split on a few hand-marked clips.

### The AI listener: useful, but not as a measure of effort

An AI reading a transcript has no memory limits and no listening effort. It will almost always recover the point, so it checks *understanding*, which fluent speakers nearly always pass. You are also the worst possible judge of listener effort, because you know what you meant. So the tap does not measure clarity. It does something else that matters: it **checks the AI's choice of "the point"**, and seconds-to-point, the headline line and the clear version all depend on that choice. Keep the restatement and the tap, and log "Not quite" rates as an eval signal. "What I didn't follow" is the riskier half. No study shows an AI can predict what a listener would miss, and audio AI models predicted human comprehensibility ratings poorly (best r = .28) ([Issa & Ali 2026](https://aclanthology.org/people/elsayed-issa/unverified/)). (**Weak**, L2 and pronunciation-focused.) Run it in the friend eval first. Show it to users only if it matches what friends actually missed.

### The three marks: keep the parts, change the display

This idea runs against the most consistent evidence in the notes. Every validated speaking rubric centres on the same core: a clearly stated central message, support, and organization with a conclusion. The top level of the AAC&U rubric is a message that is "precisely stated". The bottom level is one that "can be deduced, but is not explicitly stated" ([AAC&U VALUE rubric](https://www.assessment.aa.ufl.edu/media/assessmentaaufledu/value-rubrics/oral_communication-value_rubric.pdf)). Missing parts are penalized more reliably than extra words ([Engelhardt et al. 2006](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Engelhardt-et-al.-2006_GriceanMaximQuantity_JML.pdf)), and the order of essential parts predicted coherence ([Tsunemoto & Trofimovich 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/coherence-and-comprehensibility-in-second-language-speakers-academic-speaking-performance/85221E116322A1A7ACD84242C454712A)). The real problems with the marks lie elsewhere. They look like a grade, and grades can cancel out the benefit of comments ([Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)). Their reliability is also unproven: 65% agreement on 9 clips. "Clear point out of 6" feeds the progress chart, `weeklyFacts`, the weekly note, `tipFocus.ts` and `evals:judge`, so changing the data is not a small step. For version 1, **keep the stored 0–2 marks exactly as they are and change only the feedback screen**: show each part as a plain word ("Point: stated at 0:45", "Ending: missing") rather than a number, below the tip. That keeps the construct and drops the look of a grade. A later option is to derive the three parts from the four-label map. That would need a judge prompt change, an `evals:judge` rerun, and edits to PRODUCT.md and ARCHITECTURE.md first.

### The friend eval: the right idea, with honest limits

This is the most valuable idea on the list, because it is the only way to learn whether any measure tracks what listeners feel. Be clear about what it can show. Single raters are noisy. In a large study, trained raters agreed with each other at only ρ ≈ .39 per pair ([Uehara 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26137v1)). (**Weak**: an unreviewed preprint.) On the classroom rubric study's holistic score, a single rater's reliability was .63 ([ETS RR-15-36](https://ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2015/jvoa.html)). By the standard Spearman-Brown formula (my calculation), three friends average to roughly **.66 to .84**. That is "moderate to good", not "excellent". Published studies reach .90 with about 15 trained or 50 untrained raters ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10531380)). With 25 clips, a correlation needs to be about **r ≈ .40** before it clears chance at the usual 5% level (my calculation). Clips from one speaker also vary less than clips from many, which shrinks correlations. So the eval can catch large effects or a badly wrong AI. **It cannot fairly rank eight candidate measures against each other**: test too many and one will look good by luck. Choose a few measures to test before you look at the data.

## What to add, and the smallest version 1

The evidence supports three additions that are not on your list. **Vague-word rate** comes first: plain code, a short word list (ambiguous designation, indeterminate quantity, multiplicity), shown as one pointed question ("You said 'a lot of things' twice. Which things?"). Its evidence is lecture-based and from one lab, so treat it as a candidate. **"And"-chaining** comes second: additive connectives per 100 words, which is plain code. It rests on a single L2 study, but it is the most direct countable signature of rambling found. Compute it and test it before showing it. Third, add an eval check, not a feature: **make sure the judge does not reward short, thin answers**. AI essay scorers rate short, underdeveloped essays higher than humans do ([Mathew et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23714)). (**Weak**, written.)

Two tempting additions should stay out. A **signpost count** has evidence *against* it for short answers. An **automatic "drift" score** compares chunks of the answer with typical answers to the same question. It correlated r = .68 with human coherence ratings on 60-second spoken answers ([Hoffman et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6150697/)). (**Moderate**, healthy young and older adults.) But it would mark a vivid, useful example as drift, which is exactly the false alarm you want to avoid. At most, use it as a hidden input to the judge. Ambiguous pronouns and sentence length have only checklist-level support (**Weak**), so skip them.

The smallest evidence-backed version 1 is this. The feedback screen keeps **one tip**, chosen by code from point placement, an off-point stretch or a missing ending. Under it sits **one headline line** about where the point came, and the **AI's one-sentence restatement with the tap**. The three parts (point, support, ending) appear as words, taken from the existing stored marks, and a four-label map sits behind a tap. The Measure stage adds **mid-clause pauses**, **restarts separated from repeats**, **vague-word rate** and **"and" rate** in code. Fillers stay as a per-minute rate framed as "low is fine". Vague and "and" rates are stored but not shown until the friend eval supports them. The **friend eval runs before any new headline measure ships**. Cut for now: the full side-by-side map as the default view, AI deciding what is "noticeable", a precise "% wasted" figure, and seconds-to-point as the headline progress number.

## How to run the friend eval and check the AI judge

Run it on **25–30 clips** chosen for variety: tight, circling, point-late and story-like, a mix of Work and Everyday, plus 5–8 first-attempt-and-retry pairs. Friends should **listen to audio, not read transcripts**. Hearing someone changes how competent and thoughtful they seem compared with reading the same words ([Schroeder & Epley 2015](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797615572906)). For each clip, ask for two things that match your two-part definition. First, **"Write the main point in one sentence"**, which measures understanding. Second, **"How easy was it to follow?"** on a 1–5 scale with every step labelled (1 = no effort at all, 5 = hard work), which measures effort. Research studies usually use 9 points, but raters struggle to tell the middle steps apart, more so on 9 points than 5 ([Isaacs & Thomson 2013](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1527145/1/Isaacs_rater_experience_rating_scale_length.pdf)). With only three raters, a short labelled scale is the safer choice. Use one effort scale only, because effort measures disagree with each other ([Alhanbali et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01749/pdf)). For the retry pairs, ask a forced choice: **"Which was easier to follow?"** Comparing two answers is reportedly much more reliable for AI judges than scoring one at a time, though that figure is unverified ([Uehara 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26137v1)). Add three yes/no items: was there a reason or example, did it end clearly, and was there a stretch you'd cut. Concrete, observable items are rated more reliably than overall impressions ([ETS RR-15-36](https://ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2015/jvoa.html)).

Before the real round, rate 3–5 shared clips together and discuss them. Rater training should continue until agreement is acceptable ([Szafran 2017](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/pare/article/id/1630)). Everyone rates every clip, in shuffled order. Report the panel's agreement as an ICC (an agreement score between raters) with its 95% confidence interval, and read the *lower* bound: below .50 is poor, .50–.75 moderate, .75–.90 good ([Koo & Li 2016](https://papers.mewayz.com/paper/oa_W2327037637)). Report the AI judge's agreement against the **panel average**, not against one person, using weighted kappa (agreement that discounts lucky matches and counts near-misses as partly right). Plain percent agreement overstates quality, by 33–41 points in one large benchmark ([Norman et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)). The same work found that judges can give the same score run after run and still be biased. So "same score twice" is necessary but not enough. For the point check, have the AI and each friend write the point independently, then mark whether they name the same claim.

Set expectations for the AI from what the field has seen. AI scoring of spoken-answer transcripts reached weighted agreement of about 0.67, against 0.73 between humans ([AAAS 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.aaas-1.2.pdf)). (**Moderate, L2.**) Holistic coherence is where both humans and AI are least reliable. GPT-4 matched human coherence ratings of summaries at only ρ ≈ .58 ([G-Eval, Microsoft playbook](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/ai/playbook/technology-guidance/generative-ai/working-with-llms/evaluation/g-eval-metric-for-summarization)). Timing features track fluency well, but once overall quality is controlled for, they correlate *negatively* with purposefulness and logic ([Uehara 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26137v1)). (**Weak**, an unreviewed preprint.) So code measures cannot stand in for the judge on structure. The judge needs its own check. Then test the measures you picked in advance against the panel's ease ratings: seconds-to-point, the off-point stretch, the vague-word rate and the "and" rate. Four is the most this panel can fairly test. Keep only what clearly tracks the ratings. Clips from one speaker may not vary enough in vague words or "and" use for 25–30 clips to show a small effect. If they don't, those two stay stored and unshown, not proven useless.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the goal. "Articulate" is not mainly the absence of "um". It is giving the listener the parts they need, early enough and in an order they can follow, without vague placeholders or abandoned sentences. Most of that can be checked cheaply, and the countable parts belong in code. The ideas that sound most modern, an AI map and a "% wasted" score, are the least proven. Their numbers would look more precise than the evidence behind them. The old three marks, which you were ready to drop, are the part every rubric agrees on.

The deeper point is that your app is in a position to produce evidence nobody has. No study has tested point-first, rambling or listening effort with fluent native speakers. Thirty clips rated by three friends will not settle that question. They will tell you which one measure deserves to be the headline, and they will catch an AI that is confidently wrong. Run them before building the bigger features. Feedback built on an untested number risks being specific and wrong, which is worse than being vague.
