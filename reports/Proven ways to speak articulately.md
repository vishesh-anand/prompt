# Say the point first, then try again

The best-tested way to get more articulate on the spot is simple. You answer a question out loud, get one specific note on what to fix, and answer the same question again right away. Your app already does this. Repeating a speaking task is backed by a 2025 meta-analysis, and specific feedback that says what to do next is backed by decades of feedback research. Almost every famous coach teaches the same structure: say your point first, give one reason or example, then close. That advice is near-universal, but nobody has tested it on spoken answers. Some features that sound useful have weak support for someone who already speaks English well: pronunciation, grammar, a general "fluency score" and a 3-minute timer. Pronunciation and grammar feedback work mainly for people learning English as a second language. PRODUCT.md already rules them out for version 1. The research points to better ways to spend effort. Measure *where* pauses fall, not just how many there are. Tell restarts apart from simple repeats. Flag hedging words. Frame filler counts as "low is fine" instead of "zero is the goal." Treat improvement on new questions, not the retry, as the honest sign of progress.

**How to read the evidence tags in this report**

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | At least one meta-analysis (a study that pools many studies) |
| **Moderate** | Several studies that agree |
| **Weak** | One small study, or studies that disagree |
| **Practice** | Codified advice (Army, McKinsey, Toastmasters, coaches), never tested for speech |
| **None found** | The research team looked and found nothing |
| **L2** | The evidence comes from people speaking a second language. It may not carry over fully to native speakers |

One caveat covers the whole report. **Almost all controlled training studies on speaking fluency were done with second-language learners.** Research on native speakers is mostly about how listeners *perceive* speech, not about training. So "proven" here usually means "proven for learners, and plausible for you."

## Great communicators win on structure and plain words, not on zero "ums"

There is little careful analysis of how admired speakers talk when unscripted. The best-documented case is a useful surprise. Linguist Mark Liberman counted an "uh" or "um" **about every 19 words in one Obama interview**, against once every 117 words for Donald Trump ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.edu.au/presidential-pauses-what-those-ums-and-uhs-really-tell-us-about-candidates-for-the-white-house-204972)). Obama is still widely seen as articulate. His clarity comes from somewhere else. His speechwriter Jon Favreau described the job as explaining things "in as few words as possible" ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2013/03/reading-the-presidents-mind)). He also said to sum up the point in a few conversational sentences before speaking, and to cut anything that does not support it ([Favreau PDF](https://demofinland.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Three-lessons-in-storytelling_Jon-Favreau-English.pdf)). That is anecdote, not a study, but it fits the research below.

Feynman's famous BBC explanations start from everyday things like fire, magnets and rubber bands ([Open Culture](https://openculture.com/2024/12/richard-feynman-enthusiastically-explains-how-to-think-like-a-physicist-in-fun-to-imagine.html)). He also refused an analogy he thought was misleading ([U of Toronto essay](https://hep.physics.utoronto.ca/~pekka/pmu199y201718/ExampleEssay1.docx)). The lesson is that a concrete example helps only if it is accurate. The "Feynman technique" is a later label he never published ([Sketchplanations](https://sketchplanations.com/feynman-learning-technique)). The closest real evidence is the self-explanation effect. In a small 1994 study, students prompted to explain a text to themselves understood it better ([Chi et al. PDF](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/chiselfexplanation.pdf)). That study is about learning content, not speaking clearly. The research found no reliable analysis of the unscripted speech of Jobs, Buffett, Ardern or Nadella.

Business writing cultures say the same thing in formal terms. The US Army's **BLUF** (Bottom Line Up Front) requires the main point first ([Wikipedia: BLUF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication))). McKinsey's **Pyramid Principle** puts the answer at the top and the support underneath ([untools](https://untools.co/minto-pyramid/)). Amazon banned slides in 2004 because, Bezos argued, a written narrative "forces better thought" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/04/23/what-jeff-bezos-learned-from-requiring-6-page-memos-at-amazon.html)). Put together for a spoken answer: lead with the point, then *connect* the reason to it with words like "because" and "so," instead of listing loose fragments. These are all **Practice**. They were built for writing, and no experiment has tested answer-first against build-up for spoken answers. **This is the biggest evidence gap behind your app's "say it in your first sentence" tip.** It is still the best bet available, because everything else points the same way.

## Coaches agree on three habits, and only one drill has been tested

Matt Abrahams of Stanford teaches the most-cited method for speaking on the spot. First, calm your body with slow breaths where the out-breath is longer than the in-breath. Then lower the bar ("dare to be dull"). Listen for what is really being asked. Then answer with **What? So What? Now What?**: your point, why it matters to the listener, the next step ([Toastmasters Magazine](https://toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2023/sept/impromptu-speaking)). He also endorses PREP (Point, Reason, Example, Point) ([National CIO Review](https://nationalcioreview.com/articles-insights/master-the-moment-how-to-communicate-without-a-script/)). His practice drill is to map emails and articles you read onto that structure. Other coaches add their own versions. Chris Anderson says every TED talk needs a one-sentence **throughline** ([Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/ted-talks-the-official-guide-to-public-speaking/)). Randy Olson's **And, But, Therefore** replaces "and, and, and" lists with context, a problem and a resolution ([SESYNC](https://www.sesync.org/resources/communications-toolkit-and-therefore-statement)). Carmine Gallo argues for the rule of three, because short-term memory holds only about three chunks ([Shortform on Gallo](https://www.shortform.com/blog/effective-speech/)).

Across all these sources, coaches agree on three things. **Say the point first** is the strongest agreement. **Pause instead of filling** comes up separately from Vinh Giang ([ISACA](https://www.isaca.org/resources/news-and-trends/newsletters/atisaca/2021/volume-37/the-magic-trick-to-gaining-influence)), Jefferson Fisher ([Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/790497/the-next-conversation-workbook-by-jefferson-fisher/audio/)) and Abrahams. **Say less** comes from Fisher, Anderson and BLUF. None of the named structures has been tested on impromptu answers. They are all **Practice**.

One drill does have real evidence: **record yourself and review it**. A meta-analysis of 12 experiments found that video feedback in public speaking courses improved speech content and skills without raising anxiety ([OJP abstract](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/effect-videotape-feedback-development-presentation-skills)). The detail matters for your app. **Setting a goal before reviewing beat simply rewatching.** Self-review also made students better judges of their own speeches (two studies, 102 and 622 people) ([Missouri State](https://bearworks.missouristate.edu/articles-coal/349/)). Your loop is a record-and-review loop with a goal attached (the one tip). That is the version the evidence favors.

Toastmasters' Table Topics, the standard impromptu drill, runs **1 to 2 minutes**. A green light shows at 1:00, yellow at 1:30 and red at 2:00 ([MIT Toastmasters](https://web.mit.edu/toastmasters/roles/timer.html)). Studies of Toastmasters are small pre-and-post surveys, mostly about anxiety in second-language speakers. A 2022 review found "little academic research" on the method ([Kalińska review](https://ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1116252)). Improv training (taught at Stanford by Dan Klein) has randomized lab evidence for tolerating uncertainty and creative thinking. It has no evidence for speaking clearly ([Michigan dissertation](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/handle/2027.42/151483)). It helps you start talking. It does not organize what you say.

## Repeating the same question is the best-proven way to get better

The strongest training evidence is for **task repetition**. A 2025 meta-analysis found that repeating a speaking task gives a substantial boost to complexity and accuracy, and a smaller one to fluency. The boost shrinks when the repeats are spaced further apart ([Hildesheim record](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19120)). (**Strong, L2.**) A detailed study had 32 Japanese learners give the same talk six times. Speech rate rose most across the first three attempts. Pauses *between* clauses dropped by the 2nd attempt, and pauses *inside* clauses kept dropping until the 4th ([Lambert et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/task-repetition-and-second-language-speech-processing/0EA95A4C7D9E90CD2AB30043F84A4635)). In plain terms, the first retry mostly helps you plan and organize. Later retries help you find words. **Most of the gain comes in attempts 2 to 4.**

Do the gains carry over to new questions? There is one small but well-designed study. Twenty-four students practised the "4/3/2" drill: the same talk in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes. **Only the group that repeated the same topic kept its fluency gains on a new topic.** The group that changed topics each round did not ([de Jong & Perfetti 2011 PDF](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/perfettilab/pubpdfs/de%20Jong%20Language%20Learning.pdf)). (**Weak, L2.**) That supports retrying the *same* question, not jumping to a new one. Shrinking the time also helps. In a study of 20 students, the 4/3/2 format improved fluency more than giving the same talk three times at a fixed length ([Thai & Boers 2016](https://metatoc.com/papers/74068-repeating-a-monologue-under-increasing-time-pressure-effects-on-fluency-complexity-and-accuracy)). (**Weak, L2.**) Adding short, explicit correction after the 4/3/2 drill improved both fluency and accuracy ([Tran & Saito 2021](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10128571/1/LTR2021.pdf)). That is close to what your app does: a retry, plus a tip.

There is an honest catch. **A better second attempt is partly rehearsal.** You already know what you want to say. Your before-and-after screen shows a real effect, but it overstates lasting skill. The honest measure of progress is whether first attempts on *new* questions improve week over week. Your progress chart already tracks only first attempts. That design choice is right.

Planning time before speaking also raises fluency. In one study of 31 learners, even **1 minute of planning** improved fluency and accuracy, and more complex speech needed about 10 minutes ([Mehnert 1998](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-different-lengths-of-time-for-planning-on-second-language-performance/B828DF63B5C5ADAA2E8CCD1A03870575)). (**Moderate, L2.**) But planning makes *that one answer* better. No study shows it builds the skill of speaking *without* preparation, which is the point of your app.

Practice may also lower nerves. A meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials found psychological treatments cut fear of public speaking by a medium-to-large amount: **g = 0.74 right after treatment and g = 1.11 at follow-up** ([Ebrahimi et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6428748/)). (**Strong**, but these were mostly therapist-led exposure treatments for anxious people.) Speaking alone to a phone is a much milder form of exposure, so expect a smaller effect. No trial of solo app practice has measured anxiety.

## Where you pause matters more than how often you say "um"

Listener research shows what actually makes speech *sound* fluent. A meta-analysis found that listeners' fluency ratings track **overall speech rate (r = .76)** and **pauses in the middle of a clause (r = −.72)** most closely. Pauses *between* clauses matter less (r = −.48). Repairs and restarts matter least (r = −.20) ([Cambridge paper citing Suzuki et al. 2021](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/FAB18835B2117A299C33F8F502248C81)). (**Strong**, mostly ratings of L2 speakers.) In a follow-up study, the share of pauses that fell mid-clause was the single best predictor of a fluency rating. A pause at the end of a sentence sounds like thinking. A pause halfway through a phrase sounds like struggling. **Your app has word timings, so it can tell these apart in plain code.**

Filler research really does point both ways, and the app should say so. On one side, "uh" and "um" are real words with a job. They signal a coming delay and help listeners predict what comes next ([Clark & Fox Tree, via Stanford](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/pubs.html)). In an older study, "um" made a better impression than a silent pause, though no pause at all was best ([Christenfeld 1995](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02175503)). On the other side, a 2024 study had people rate speeches with 0, 5 or 12 fillers a minute. **Twelve a minute hurt the ratings. Five or fewer did not** ([Behaviorist Book Club summary of Laske et al. 2024](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/aba-fundamentals/um--so--like--do-speech-disfluencies-matter--a-parametric-evaluation-o/)). (**Weak**: one study, seen only through a summary.) Another study found filled pauses in spontaneous speech made speakers seem less ready and less certain ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8579j2fh)). The fair conclusion is that a low filler rate is fine and zero is not the goal. **Check the Laske paper itself before showing "5 a minute" to users.**

Two more cues have solid tests behind them. First, **restarts cost listeners more than repeats.** Listeners process a repeated word ("the gift the gift department") about as fast as fluent speech ([Fox Tree 1995](https://doi.org/10.1006/jmla.1995.1032)). Abandoning a sentence and starting it over causes more words to be missed ([Lickley & Bard 1996](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_1996/lickley96_icslp.html)) and a "re-analysis" response in the brain ([McAllister et al. 2001](https://www.isca-archive.org/diss_2001/mcallister01_diss.html)). (**Moderate.**) Second, **hedges lower how authoritative you sound.** Phrases like "kind of," "I guess" and "sort of" lowered perceived authority in several experiments ([Hosman 1989](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16291); [Hosman & Wright 1987](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16292)). A meta-analysis found that powerless language tends to reduce persuasion ([Durik et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%206/DurikETAL2008.pdf)). (**Moderate.**) The effect depends on how expert the speaker already seems ([Smith et al. 1998](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16286)). Some hedges are simply honest. That makes hedging something to point out, not something to score.

Structure words also help. In a classic lecture study, **"big-picture" signposts** ("the first point is," "now let's turn to") helped listeners remember more than small fillers like "so" or "well" ([Chaudron & Richards, cited in a UEW thesis](https://ir.uew.edu.gh/handle/123456789/1618)). (**Moderate, L2 listeners.**) Concrete words are understood and remembered better than abstract ones ([Richardson 2003](https://oro.open.ac.uk/927)), though that evidence comes from reading, not listening. Speaking speed has **no proven ideal**. Only extremes clearly matter, and the popular "120 to 160 words a minute" range has no source.

There is one more point about your filler list. It mixes true filled pauses (um, uh) with discourse markers (like, basically, actually, you know, and "so" at the start of a sentence). The research above is mostly about um and uh. "So" at the start of a sentence can work as a signpost.

## Feedback works when it is one specific next step about the task

Feedback is not automatically good. A huge review of 607 effect sizes found an average benefit (d = 0.41), but **more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse**. Feedback backfired when it pulled attention toward the person (praise, grades, comparisons with others) and away from the task ([Kluger & DeNisi abstract](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc)). (**Strong.**) Valerie Shute's review gives the practical rules ([Shute 2008 PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)). Focus on the task. Explain what, how and why. Give feedback in small pieces, because too much causes overload and gets ignored. Give it after the attempt, not during. Use praise "sparingly, if at all." Compare people with their own past, not with others. Avoid overall grades. In one study she cites, students who got only comments improved a lot. Students who got grades plus comments did not improve at all. Your app's one tip, its no-generic-praise voice and its self-comparison already follow these rules. The clear-point marks out of 6 are the one place that looks like a grade. They should stay visually below the tip.

The "better version" is close to what language teachers call a **recast**: your own words, said correctly. In a study of 105 learners, recasts helped people notice the gap between what they said and the target ([Choo et al. 2021](https://jurnal.usk.ac.id/SiELE/article/view/18546)). But learners often fail to see a recast as a correction at all ([Asian EFL Journal](https://www.asian-efl-journal.com/main-editions-new/do-recasts-promote-noticing-the-gap-in-l2-learning/)). Prompts that push the learner to fix it themselves beat recasts in a meta-analysis of 15 classroom studies ([Lyster & Saito 2010](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)). (**Strong, L2, about grammar.**) Your design of a rewrite, plus one explicit tip, plus a retry covers both. Showing *what changed* in the rewrite (for example, "your point moved to the front") would help users see it as a correction. That last idea is untested. Unaided self-review is weak: in one study, trainees found only about **25% of their own errors** when they reviewed transcripts alone ([Salas 2015](https://revistas.udistrital.edu.co/index.php/calj/article/view/4989)). This supports pointing to specific moments rather than just offering replay.

## Pronunciation and grammar scoring fit learners, not fluent speakers

Stimuler and similar apps score pronunciation, grammar, vocabulary and fluency ([Stimuler blog](https://stimuler.tech/blog/best-english-fluency-app)). Automated pronunciation feedback does work for English learners. A meta-analysis found a medium effect (**g = 0.69**). The effect was large for individual sounds and small for stress and intonation, and **practising alone gave a small effect** ([Ngo, Chen & Lai 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D/S0958344023000113a.pdf/the-effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-meta-analysis.pdf)). (**Strong, L2.**) The machine scores themselves are shaky. One study put agreement between machine and human pronunciation scores at only about r = .61 ([SAGE](https://doi.org/10.1177/02655322251387816)). Stimuler's own Google Play reviews complain of "inaccurate assessments of speaking abilities" ([Kimola analysis](https://kimola.com/reports/unlock-insights-english-speaking-app-user-feedback-analysis-google-play-156230)).

For a fluent English speaker whose problem is rambling, none of this addresses the actual problem. The research found **no evidence on "vocabulary precision"** at all. The one word-level feature with tests behind it is hedging. And a vague "fluency score" adds little that pause location, restarts and filler rate don't already measure better. PRODUCT.md already lists accent and grammar coaching under "Not in version 1." The evidence agrees. **One fact would change this: if most beta testers speak English as a second language,** pronunciation and grammar feedback becomes a real later idea worth testing. It stays out of version 1 either way.

The app market tells the same story. Nearly every app counts fillers and pace: Yoodli, Orai, Poised, Speeko and Microsoft's Speaker Coach ([Outdoo on Yoodli](https://www.outdoo.ai/blog/yoodli-review-and-pricing); [Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/powerpoint/suggestions-from-speaker-coach)). The research found **no app that combines content structure, one ranked tip, a rewrite of your own answer and an immediate retry.** That combination is your app's edge. More delivery metrics would make it look like everyone else. No independent trial shows that any of these apps improves how audiences rate people's speaking.

## On timer length, the evidence supports 90 seconds to 2 minutes, not 3

No study sets an ideal length for an impromptu answer. Practice gives the only guide: Table Topics runs 1 to 2 minutes, with the green light at 1 minute as the minimum ([MIT Toastmasters](https://web.mit.edu/toastmasters/roles/timer.html)). A 60-second cap sits at the bottom of that range. It fits point, reason and close, but it leaves little room for a real example. A product reason also points toward going longer. ARCHITECTURE.md notes the judge scores an answer cut off by the timer as 0 on "ending," even when the speaker was wrapping up. A slightly longer cap means fewer cut-offs. Against that, coaches all push brevity, and longer recordings cost more to transcribe and judge. **Three minutes has no support** for an impromptu answer, and it invites the rambling the app exists to fix. One idea from the 4/3/2 research is worth testing: keep the first attempt at its full length and make the retry shorter (for example, 90 then 60 seconds). The research suggests this pushes speakers to be more compact. It has not been tested with native speakers.

## Conclusion

The research changes the picture in one important way. "Articulate" is mostly about **organization and where you hesitate**, not about the polish features that language apps sell. The tested parts of your app (retry, one specific tip, comparing against your own past) are already in place. The best next steps are cheap plain-code measures from timings you already have: pause location, restarts and hedges. Each of these has better evidence than a filler count. The weakest link is the most-repeated advice of all. "Say your point first" has never been tested in speech. Your own data could fill that gap. Track whether answers with an early main point go with better scores on later new questions. That would turn the app's core tip from consensus into evidence.

A second implication: almost every training finding here comes from second-language learners. So the beta itself is the first real test for native speakers. Watch first-attempt trends on new questions and the "This feedback is wrong" rate. Those two signals will tell you more than adding pronunciation, grammar or a longer timer would.
